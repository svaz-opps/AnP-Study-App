## 6.1 Skin layers and their cell types

`ch6-layers`

**Focus:**

- Order epidermal strata from basale to corneum; lucidum is specific to thick skin.
- Distinguish epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis.

Skin consists of epidermis and dermis; hypodermis lies beneath and is not usually counted as skin proper. Epidermis is avascular keratinized stratified squamous epithelium. Dermis is vascular connective tissue supporting the epidermis, sensory structures, follicles, glands, and vessels. Hypodermis contains areolar/adipose tissue that anchors, cushions, stores fuel, and insulates.

From deep to superficial, epidermal strata are basale, spinosum, granulosum, lucidum in thick skin only, and corneum. Basal division replenishes keratinocytes that move outward, differentiate, accumulate keratin and barrier lipids, lose organelles, and eventually shed. Palms and soles have thick skin and a distinct lucidum, but lack hair follicles. Most other locations have thin skin without a distinct lucidum.

Keratinocytes build the barrier. Melanocytes produce melanin and transfer it to neighboring cells. Epidermal dendritic/Langerhans cells participate in immune surveillance. Tactile/Merkel cells associate with sensory endings. Differences in pigmentation involve melanin production, type, distribution, and persistence rather than a simple difference in melanocyte counts. The epidermal barrier reduces water loss but is not an absolutely impermeable wall.

## 6.2 Dermal structure, sensation, and skin functions

`ch6-dermis`

**Focus:**

- Papillary dermis is areolar; reticular dermis is dense irregular connective tissue.
- Compare superficial tactile receptors with deeper lamellated corpuscles.

Papillary dermis is a superficial areolar layer with dermal papillae, capillary loops, and tactile receptors. Reticular dermis contains interwoven collagen and elastic fibers providing tensile strength and recoil. The hypodermis adds insulation and mobility over deeper structures. Dermal vessels nourish epidermis by diffusion because the epidermis itself lacks vessels.

Tactile/Meissner corpuscles in dermal papillae detect light touch and low-frequency vibration in glabrous skin. Lamellated/Pacinian corpuscles lie deeper in dermis or subcutis and detect vibration and changing deep pressure. Free nerve endings contribute pain and temperature sensation. The onion-like lamellae of a Pacinian corpuscle distinguish it from a superficial tactile corpuscle.

Skin functions include physical/chemical protection, water conservation, immune surveillance, sensation, temperature regulation, a vascular reservoir, and vitamin D precursor synthesis initiated by ultraviolet exposure. Sweat excretion is minor compared with renal waste excretion. Skin immune cells release signals that coordinate defense; skin does not replace bone marrow as the principal blood-cell production site. Barrier disruption can impair fluid balance and infection resistance even when underlying muscle remains intact.

## 6.3 Hair, nails, and sebaceous glands

`ch6-accessory`

**Focus:**

- Hair/nails arise from epidermal cells despite follicle structures extending into dermis.
- Arrector pili is smooth muscle; sebaceous secretion is holocrine.

Hair and nails are keratinized epidermal derivatives. In hair follicles, dividing matrix cells in the bulb produce the hair; the dermal papilla provides vascular support. The root lies below the surface, while the shaft extends beyond it. The follicle surrounds the root. Pigment contributes hair color; the exposed shaft consists largely of dead keratinized cells.

Arrector pili are smooth muscle bundles associated with hair follicles. Their contraction raises hairs and creates goosebumps during cold or emotional responses. They are not skeletal muscle, and piloerection contributes little insulation in sparsely haired humans. Nails grow from the nail matrix; the nail plate protects digit ends and assists manipulation. The lunula is a visible pale region near the proximal nail, not the only location of all nail growth.

Sebaceous glands usually empty sebum into follicles and use holocrine secretion: whole cells contribute to the product. Sebum lubricates skin and hair. Some sebaceous glands open directly onto the surface, so association with follicles is common rather than universal. Acne involves follicular plugging, sebum, microbial interactions, and inflammation; it is not simply dirt trapped on otherwise normal skin.

## 6.4 Eccrine, apocrine, and modified glands

`ch6-sweat`

**Focus:**

- Eccrine glands open to surface and support evaporative cooling.
- Apocrine sweat commonly drains into follicles; bacterial metabolism contributes odor.

Eccrine sweat glands are coiled tubular glands whose ducts open onto the skin surface. They release a watery secretion containing salts and small quantities of wastes by merocrine exocytosis. They are widely distributed and important for temperature regulation, especially during heat exposure and activity. Cooling occurs when sweat evaporates, not merely when liquid appears on skin.

Apocrine sweat glands are concentrated in regions such as axillae and groin, commonly drain into follicles, and become more active around puberty. Their secretion contains additional organic components; bacterial metabolism produces much associated odor. The secretion itself is initially relatively odorless. Emotional influences are important. The historical name apocrine does not establish the exact mechanism of every released component in human sweat glands.

Ceruminous glands of the external auditory canal contribute to earwax, and mammary glands produce milk; both are modified cutaneous glands. Contrast sweat with sebum: sebaceous glands release oily material through whole-cell breakdown, whereas eccrine glands release watery sweat without sacrificing whole cells. Predict whether a gland contributes primarily to evaporative cooling, lubrication, or specialized secretion from its structure and outlet.

## 6.5 Heat production, transfer, and feedback

`ch6-temperature`

**Focus:**

- Separate hypothalamic control from skin vessel and sweat responses.
- Evaporation depends on environmental conditions, including humidity.

Body heat largely comes from metabolism, including active muscle. The hypothalamus integrates temperature signals and coordinates responses. Skin vessels change heat transfer rather than serving as the primary control center. Vasodilation increases cutaneous blood flow and often heat loss when the environment is cooler; vasoconstriction limits this transfer. Shivering increases skeletal muscle heat production.

Radiation transfers infrared energy without direct contact. Conduction transfers heat through contact. Convection carries heat in moving air or water. Evaporation removes heat as liquid becomes vapor. Humidity reduces evaporative effectiveness, and air hotter than skin may add heat by convection. Sweating therefore does not guarantee cooling in every environment.

Hyperthermia reflects heat gain/production exceeding dissipation without a regulated febrile set-point increase. Fever involves altered thermoregulatory signaling and is a different process. Hypothermia reflects abnormally low core temperature. Explain a temperature problem through heat balance, vascular responses, evaporation, and behavior rather than assuming that a single visible sign determines the cause. These principles are for course learning, not a substitute for clinical assessment or treatment protocols.

## 6.6 Pigmentation, defense, and skin disorders

`ch6-pigment`

**Focus:**

- Distinguish melanin, carotene, hemoglobin, and bilirubin contributions.
- Link pressure/shear to tissue ischemia and UV exposure to DNA damage risk.

Melanin contributes pigmentation and absorbs some ultraviolet radiation; it does not eliminate UV damage. Genetics, ultraviolet exposure, hormones, and selected drugs affect pigmentation. Carotene can contribute a yellow-orange tone. Dermal hemoglobin and perfusion affect redness and pallor. Cyanosis relates to altered oxygenation and hemoglobin state; jaundice reflects bilirubin accumulation. Skin appearance varies with baseline pigmentation and lighting, so color alone is an incomplete assessment.

Pressure injury develops when sustained pressure and shear impair tissue perfusion, often over bony prominences. Inflammation involves vascular and immune signaling and may cause warmth, redness, swelling, and pain; it is not identical to infection. Ultraviolet radiation contributes to many cutaneous cancers. Basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma arise from different cell populations; melanoma can spread aggressively.

Skin aging may reduce elasticity, alter barrier repair, and change glandular function. Vitiligo involves loss of pigment-producing cells in affected areas; albinism involves inherited deficits in melanin production. Avoid conflating pigment changes with vascular changes: increased melanin is not simply increased blood flow, and reduced perfusion is not reduced pigment synthesis.

## 6.7 Wound healing and burns

`ch6-repair`

**Focus:**

- Compare superficial, partial-thickness, and full-thickness depth and regenerative capacity.
- Rule of nines estimates adult surface area, not burn depth.

A superficial epidermal injury may heal through basal epithelial proliferation without substantial scarring. A deeper injury can involve hemostasis, inflammation, migration and proliferation of repair cells, new extracellular matrix, re-epithelialization, and remodeling. Fibroblasts produce collagen; new vessels support repair. Scabs and clot provide temporary protection, while scar tissue may differ from original tissue in strength and function.

Superficial burns, traditionally first degree, affect epidermis. Partial-thickness burns, traditionally second degree, extend into dermis with variable depth and often blistering; surviving epithelial appendages can contribute regeneration. Full-thickness burns, traditionally third degree, destroy the full skin thickness and many regenerative structures; extensive wounds cannot simply regenerate normal skin from their center. Severe burns disrupt fluid balance, protection, and temperature regulation.

The adult rule of nines estimates surface area: each arm 9%, each leg 18%, anterior trunk 18%, posterior trunk 18%, head/neck 9%, and perineum 1%. Children have different proportions and require age-appropriate methods. This surface estimate does not measure depth or replace a clinical severity assessment. Healing depends on injury depth, extent, blood supply, infection, nutrition, and other factors.
