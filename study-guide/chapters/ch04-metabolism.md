## 4.1 Metabolism, enzymes, and pathway control

`ch4-enzymes`

**Focus:**

- Explain active site specificity, cofactors/coenzymes, and feedback inhibition.
- Enzymes lower activation energy and are reused.

Metabolism includes anabolic synthesis and catabolic breakdown. Dehydration reactions build many macromolecules, while hydrolysis breaks many biological linkages. A reaction pathway links products of one step to substrates of the next. Each step needs an appropriate catalyst; an enzyme acting on a sugar does not automatically catalyze fatty acid breakdown.

Enzymes lower activation energy without being consumed or changing a reaction's equilibrium. The active site binds particular substrates; induced fit refines the simple lock-and-key model by allowing conformational adjustment. Names often end in -ase: lipase acts on lipid, sucrase on sucrose, and DNA polymerase synthesizes DNA. Cofactors include metal ions; coenzymes are organic helpers, often derived from vitamins. Not all enzymes require a vitamin, and a whole vitamin is not necessarily identical to its active coenzyme derivative.

Substrate and enzyme concentration, pH, temperature, and inhibitors influence rates. Extreme conditions can denature proteins. In feedback inhibition, a pathway's final product reduces an earlier reaction's activity, conserving resources. Distinguish inhibition of enzyme activity from destruction of the enzyme, and regulation of a pathway from a change in the genetic code.

## 4.2 ATP, phosphorylation, and redox

`ch4-atp`

**Focus:**

- Oxidation loses electrons; reduction gains electrons.
- Contrast substrate-level and oxidative phosphorylation.

ATP contains adenine, ribose, and three phosphate groups. ATP + water → ADP + inorganic phosphate can release free energy overall and drive coupled work. ADP + phosphate → ATP requires energy. Phosphorylation transfers or adds a phosphate group to a molecule. The ATP pool turns over rapidly, linking fuel oxidation to transport, contraction, and synthesis.

Breaking a bond requires energy; the favorable overall hydrolysis reaction reflects the properties and interactions of all reactants and products. Avoid describing the act of bond breaking alone as an energy source. Substrate-level phosphorylation transfers phosphate directly to ADP. Oxidative phosphorylation links electron transfer and a proton gradient to ATP synthase.

Oxidation loses electrons; reduction gains electrons. NAD+ and FAD accept electrons during fuel processing, becoming NADH and FADH2. They later donate electrons, regenerating oxidized carriers. In aerobic respiration glucose is oxidized and oxygen reduced to water. Unlike uncontrolled combustion, cellular oxidation occurs through enzyme-controlled steps that permit energy capture. An enzyme changes rate, whereas an electron carrier transfers reducing equivalents; these are distinct roles.

## 4.3 Glycolysis and lactate formation

`ch4-glycolysis`

**Focus:**

- One glucose yields two pyruvate, two net ATP, and two NADH in glycolysis.
- Lactate formation regenerates NAD+ without adding extra ATP.

Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol and converts one six-carbon glucose into two three-carbon pyruvate molecules. It invests two ATP and generates four, giving a net yield of two ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation. It also yields two NADH. Oxygen is not directly required by glycolysis, but oxidized NAD+ must remain available.

When oxidative metabolism cannot reoxidize NADH quickly enough, lactate dehydrogenase transfers electrons from NADH to pyruvate, producing lactate and regenerating NAD+. This regeneration permits glycolysis to continue. Lactate formation itself produces no additional ATP beyond glycolysis. Red blood cells rely on glycolysis because they lack mitochondria; working muscle can increase lactate production even when some oxygen remains available.

Lactate is a usable metabolic intermediate rather than simply a toxic waste. It can be oxidized elsewhere or contribute to glucose production. Delayed muscle soreness after unfamiliar exercise is not explained by lactate persisting for days. Yeast can regenerate NAD+ through ethanol fermentation; this is not the normal human pathway. Distinguish lack of direct oxygen use from complete independence of all other metabolic conditions.

## 4.4 Pyruvate oxidation, Krebs cycle, and electron transport

`ch4-aerobic`

**Focus:**

- Locate each stage and account for carbon, electron carriers, and ATP.
- Oxygen is the final acceptor; the proton gradient powers ATP synthase.

Pyruvate enters mitochondria and is converted to two-carbon acetyl-CoA. Per glucose, this conversion releases two CO2 and produces two NADH. The citric acid or Krebs cycle occurs in the matrix: each acetyl group joins oxaloacetate, which is regenerated. Per glucose, the cycle yields four CO2, six NADH, two FADH2, and two ATP/GTP equivalents.

The electron transport chain is embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Electrons from reduced carriers move through protein complexes, and released energy pumps protons into the intermembrane space. Protons return to the matrix through ATP synthase, driving ATP synthesis. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor and water forms. If electron transfer stops, carriers remain reduced and oxidative ATP production falls; the cycle depends indirectly on oxygen to regenerate oxidized carriers.

| Stage | Location | Key output per glucose |
|---|---|---|
| Glycolysis | Cytosol | 2 net ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate |
| Pyruvate oxidation | Mitochondrial matrix | 2 NADH, 2 CO2, 2 acetyl-CoA |
| Krebs cycle | Matrix | 2 ATP/GTP, 6 NADH, 2 FADH2, 4 CO2 |
| Oxidative phosphorylation | Inner membrane | Most aerobic ATP and water |

Modern estimates often total about 30–32 ATP per glucose, varying with shuttles and coupling. Older course accounting uses 36–38 and older carrier yields. Keep the accounting convention explicit; do not mix estimates or make a disputed total the answer to an unqualified practice question.

Further explanation: [Molecular Biology of the Cell: mitochondrial energy conversion](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26894/).

## 4.5 Fuel storage and metabolic integration

`ch4-fuels`

**Focus:**

- Compare glycogen storage with triglyceride reserve fuel.
- Fatty acids and amino acid carbon skeletons enter shared energy pathways.

Glucose can be oxidized immediately or stored as glycogen, particularly in liver and skeletal muscle. With sustained surplus energy, carbon can also contribute to fat synthesis. Fats provide the largest concentrated reserve fuel. Lipolysis releases glycerol and fatty acids; beta-oxidation converts fatty acids into acetyl-CoA while producing electron carriers. Glycerol can enter carbohydrate pathways.

Proteins have structural and functional priorities but can contribute energy. Removing amino groups permits carbon skeletons to enter metabolic pathways; nitrogen must be handled and excreted. Humans cannot synthesize every amino acid, so converting excess carbohydrate into amino acids does not eliminate the need for dietary essential amino acids.

Pathways share intermediates, yet each step remains enzyme-specific. When energy demand rises, cells draw on available fuels and regulate flux. Oxygen availability, tissue type, hormonal signals, and nutrition influence which fuels are used. The ATP yield from a fatty acid depends on chain length and accounting assumptions; learn the pathway relationships rather than transferring one historical value to every lipid. Anabolism and catabolism occur together across the body, connected by energy transfer and common intermediates.

## 4.6 DNA structure, genes, and replication

`ch4-replication`

**Focus:**

- Replication makes DNA from DNA and preserves one old strand per daughter helix.
- Separate replication from transcription and mutation from an inevitable disease outcome.

A gene is a DNA region whose expression contributes to a functional RNA or protein product. Human protein-coding gene estimates are roughly twenty thousand, while gene counts depend on definitions and annotation. DNA's sugar-phosphate backbone is covalently bonded; complementary bases pair A–T and C–G between antiparallel strands.

DNA replication occurs during S phase before mitosis. Helicase separates strands; primase supplies primers; DNA polymerases extend new DNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction using complementary templates. The leading strand is synthesized continuously relative to fork movement, while the lagging strand uses fragments joined by ligase. Each daughter double helix retains one parental strand and one newly synthesized strand: **semiconservative** replication.

Replication copies a genome for cell division; transcription copies selected information into RNA. They differ in product, enzymes, and purpose. Polymerase proofreading and repair reduce copying errors, but some mutations persist. A mutation is a change in DNA sequence. Depending on location and sequence, it can have no detectable effect, alter a protein, change expression, or contribute to disease. Not every mutation changes one amino acid or causes cancer.

## 4.7 Transcription, translation, and the genetic code

`ch4-expression`

**Focus:**

- Transcription makes RNA; translation builds a polypeptide.
- Read mRNA codons in the correct direction and distinguish template from coding DNA.

During transcription, RNA polymerase uses a DNA template to synthesize RNA. In a typical nucleated human cell this occurs in the nucleus. Messenger RNA processing includes a cap, poly-A tail, and removal of introns by splicing. Mature mRNA passes through nuclear pores to the cytoplasm. RNA uses uracil in place of thymine.

Translation occurs at ribosomes. A codon is a three-base mRNA unit; a transfer RNA anticodon pairs with it and delivers an amino acid. Ribosomal RNA contributes both structure and catalytic function. Translation generally begins at AUG, establishes a reading frame, elongates through peptide bond formation, and ends at a stop codon. Stop codons do not normally code for an additional amino acid.

For a template DNA sequence written 3′-TAC GGA ATT-5′, the mRNA is 5′-AUG CCU UAA-3′: methionine, proline, stop. Always identify whether a supplied DNA sequence is template or coding and its direction before using a code chart. Protein synthesis is essential because enzymes, receptors, transporters, and structural proteins determine cellular capabilities. DNA remains a template; it does not travel to each ribosome to become protein.
