## 3.1 The fluid mosaic membrane and cell junctions

`ch3-membrane`

**Focus:**

- Relate selective permeability to solute size, charge, polarity, and transport proteins.
- Tight junctions seal, desmosomes anchor, gap junctions communicate.

The plasma membrane is a phospholipid bilayer containing proteins, cholesterol, and surface carbohydrates. Polar heads contact extracellular fluid and cytosol; nonpolar tails face inward. Small nonpolar molecules such as oxygen cross the lipid region relatively readily. Ions and many polar solutes require channels or carriers. Selective permeability permits some substances more readily than others.

Integral proteins are embedded in the membrane and may span it; peripheral proteins associate with a surface. Functions include transport, enzymatic activity, adhesion, receptors, and recognition. Glycoprotein and glycolipid carbohydrate chains on the extracellular face contribute to the glycocalyx and recognition of cell identity. A receptor changes cell behavior when an appropriate signal binds.

Tight junctions restrict movement between neighboring epithelial cells. Desmosomes anchor adjacent cells to resist mechanical stress. Gap junctions connect cytoplasm through channels, allowing ions and small signaling molecules to pass. These structures serve different purposes: sealing, anchoring, and communication. A membrane is dynamic, but its components are organized rather than randomly interchangeable with cell wall materials; human cells do not have cellulose cell walls.

## 3.2 Organelles and the protein shipping pathway

`ch3-organelles`

**Focus:**

- Trace a secreted protein from ribosome through ER, Golgi, and vesicles.
- Distinguish lysosomal digestion, peroxisomal oxidation, and mitochondrial ATP production.

Ribosomes assemble amino acids into polypeptides. Free ribosomes commonly make cytosolic proteins; ribosomes attached to rough endoplasmic reticulum produce proteins entering the secretory or membrane pathway. Rough ER consists of membrane-bound sacs called cisternae. Smooth ER lacks attached ribosomes and contributes to lipid synthesis, detoxification, and calcium storage in specialized cells.

The Golgi apparatus receives transport vesicles, modifies and sorts proteins/lipids, and packages products into vesicles. A vesicle is a small membrane-bound transport compartment. A secreted protein generally travels ribosome → rough ER → transport vesicle → Golgi → secretory vesicle → plasma membrane. Cisternae occur in both ER and Golgi; cristae specifically describe inner mitochondrial membrane folds.

Lysosomes contain hydrolytic enzymes that digest engulfed material and worn components. Autophagy recycles cellular components; extensive self-digestion is associated with autolysis after severe damage. Peroxisomes participate in oxidation and detoxification and contain catalase to handle hydrogen peroxide; liver and kidney cells have prominent detoxification roles. Mitochondria support aerobic ATP production with a matrix and folded inner membrane. A large need for secretion predicts abundant rough ER/Golgi, whereas high energy demand predicts many mitochondria.

## 3.3 Nucleus, cytoskeleton, and surface extensions

`ch3-nucleus`

**Focus:**

- Nuclear pores regulate exchange; the nucleolus assembles ribosomal subunits.
- Microvilli increase area, cilia move surface material, a sperm flagellum propels the cell.

The nucleus contains most cellular DNA within a double-membrane envelope. Nuclear pores regulate traffic such as RNA export and protein import. The nucleolus assembles ribosomal subunits using ribosomal RNA and proteins. Chromatin is DNA associated with proteins; a nucleosome is DNA wrapped around histone proteins. Before division, chromatin condenses into visibly distinct chromosomes.

The cytoskeleton supports shape, transport, and movement. Microfilaments include actin; intermediate filaments provide mechanical strength; microtubules form intracellular tracks, spindle elements, and the core of cilia/flagella. Centrosomal machinery helps organize microtubules during division. Mature cell specialization can alter organelle composition, as in red blood cells lacking a nucleus.

Microvilli are actin-supported projections that increase absorptive surface area, such as in intestinal cells. Cilia generally move material along a surface, such as mucus in respiratory passages or fluid near uterine tubes. A flagellum propels a sperm cell. Motile cilia and flagella have microtubule-based structures. Do not assign the function of surface-area amplification to every projection: a moving cilium and an absorptive microvillus have different internal structures and roles.

## 3.4 Passive transport: diffusion and filtration

`ch3-diffusion`

**Focus:**

- Diffusion follows a concentration gradient; filtration follows a pressure gradient.
- Facilitated diffusion uses proteins but remains passive.

Simple diffusion is net movement down a concentration gradient due to molecular motion. Oxygen and carbon dioxide can diffuse through cell membranes. The rate increases with a steeper gradient, higher temperature, larger surface area, and shorter distance; larger particles generally move more slowly. Diffusion does not require cellular ATP, and individual molecules continue moving at equilibrium even when net flux is zero.

Facilitated diffusion also moves down an electrochemical gradient without direct ATP input, but uses a channel or carrier. Carrier-mediated movement is selective and can saturate when transporters are occupied. Glucose enters many cells through glucose transporters; insulin increases transporter availability in selected tissues. Some other glucose transport is coupled to ion gradients, so not all glucose transport is the same mechanism.

Filtration moves water and small solutes under a pressure gradient across a barrier. Renal glomerular filtration is driven principally by blood pressure and limited by the filtration barrier; it is not a mechanism by which whole blood cells routinely pass into urine. Passive means no direct metabolic energy expenditure by the transport process. It does not mean that blood pressure, membrane proteins, or prior physiological activity have no role.

## 3.5 Osmosis, tonicity, and cell volume

`ch3-osmosis`

**Focus:**

- Hypertonic shrinks cells; hypotonic swells cells; isotonic preserves volume.
- Tonicity depends on effective, relatively nonpenetrating solutes.

Osmosis is net water movement across a selectively permeable membrane in response to a difference in effective solute concentration. Water moves toward the side with more nonpenetrating solute when other forces do not balance it. Aquaporins facilitate water movement, but water transport remains passive.

An isotonic solution produces no sustained net change in cell volume. In a hypertonic solution, extracellular effective solute concentration is higher: water leaves and the cell shrinks, a red-cell change called crenation. In a hypotonic solution, water enters and the cell swells; excessive swelling can cause lysis. Red blood cell rupture is hemolysis.

Tonicity depends on solutes that do not freely cross the relevant membrane and therefore differs from total osmolarity. An iso-osmotic solution need not be isotonic if its solute enters cells rapidly. Predict direction first by comparing effective concentrations, then predict cell volume. Do not reverse the water direction by imagining that water is moving along the solute's concentration gradient. In an isotonic environment water still crosses in both directions; the average fluxes balance.

## 3.6 Active transport and vesicular transport

`ch3-active`

**Focus:**

- The sodium–potassium pump uses ATP and maintains ion gradients.
- Distinguish phagocytosis, pinocytosis, receptor-mediated uptake, exocytosis, and transcytosis.

Primary active transport uses ATP directly to move substances against electrochemical gradients. The sodium–potassium pump exports three sodium ions and imports two potassium ions per ATP cycle, supporting ion gradients. Secondary active transport couples one solute's downhill movement to another's uphill movement; the supporting gradient is ultimately energy-dependent.

Endocytosis brings material into membrane-bound vesicles. Phagocytosis engulfs large particles such as bacteria; pinocytosis samples extracellular fluid and dissolved substances; receptor-mediated endocytosis concentrates selected ligands through receptors. A phagosome can fuse with lysosomes for digestion. The plasma membrane supplies the initial engulfing boundary, not the nuclear membrane.

Exocytosis releases vesicle contents when the vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane, as during hormone or neurotransmitter secretion. Transcytosis combines uptake on one cell surface, vesicle movement, and release from another. Vesicular processes require energy and cytoskeletal/trafficking machinery, even though a concentration gradient alone does not describe the movement of a large particle. Distinguish secretion by exocytosis from passive leakage through a channel.

## 3.7 Cell cycle, mitosis, and cytokinesis

`ch3-cycle`

**Focus:**

- DNA replication occurs in S phase; sister chromatids separate in anaphase.
- Mitosis divides nuclear material; cytokinesis divides cytoplasm.

Interphase occupies much of the cycle. In G1, cells grow and perform normal functions. S phase duplicates DNA. G2 prepares for division and checks replicated material. A duplicated chromosome has two sister chromatids joined at a centromere; DNA replication precedes mitosis rather than occurring during metaphase.

During prophase, chromosomes condense and spindle assembly begins; nuclear envelope breakdown follows in the prometaphase subdivision often included within prophase in a four-phase course scheme. Metaphase aligns chromosomes at the equator. Anaphase separates sister chromatids as centromeric cohesion is released. Telophase forms new nuclei as chromosomes decondense. Cytokinesis divides cytoplasm, usually through an actin-based cleavage furrow in animal cells.

Mitosis distributes duplicated chromosomes into daughter nuclei; cytokinesis physically partitions the cell. Normal mitotic division generally preserves chromosome number. Cells may leave the cycle into G0. Mature skeletal muscle fibers and most mature neurons have very limited division; mature red cells cannot divide because they lack nuclei. Some specialized tissues rely on stem or progenitor cells for replacement. Distinguish a nondividing mature cell from a tissue that still contains dividing replacement cells.

## 3.8 Division control, stem cells, and cancer

`ch3-differentiation`

**Focus:**

- Different gene expression drives much cellular specialization.
- Distinguish self-renewal, differentiation, benign growth, invasion, and metastasis.

Cell division supports growth, replacement, and repair. Checkpoints monitor DNA integrity, replication, and chromosome attachment. Cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases help coordinate progression. Growth factors, nutrient supply, contact with neighboring cells, and internal damage signals influence whether a cell divides. Apoptosis removes selected damaged or unnecessary cells through regulated death.

Stem cells can self-renew and produce differentiated descendants. Progenitor cells generally have more limited developmental potential. Differentiation largely reflects differences in gene expression: most nucleated somatic cells contain similar genetic information but activate different sets of genes. A nerve cell and a skin cell usually do not differ because each possesses entirely unrelated genomes.

Cancer involves loss of normal growth regulation and may include invasion and metastasis. A benign tumor remains localized and does not metastasize, although location can still make it harmful. A malignant tumor can invade nearby tissues and spread. DNA mutations can affect growth-regulating genes; chemical carcinogens, radiation, inherited variants, and some infections contribute in different contexts. An exposure is a risk factor, not proof that every exposed cell becomes cancerous. Normal healing requires regulated cell growth and differs from uncontrolled proliferation.
