## 8.1 What Is a Joint?

`ch8-overview`

A **joint** (also called an **articulation**) is a place where two or more bones meet. Joints hold the skeleton together, but they also allow movement.

Joints can be classified in two ways. The two systems describe the same joints from different angles:

- **Functional classification**: based on **how much movement** the joint allows.
- **Structural classification**: based on **the material that joins the bones** and whether a joint cavity is present.

> **Study tip** (tip)
>
> Think of **function** as "what does it do?" (movement) and **structure** as "what is it made of?" (fibrous tissue, cartilage, or a fluid-filled capsule).

**Functional classification**

Describing how much movement a joint allows is functional. Structural classification describes the tissue that joins the bones.

</details>

## 8.2 Functional Classification of Joints

`ch8-functional`

**Focus:**

- Joints with **some limited degree of movement** are **amphiarthrotic**.
- Synarthrotic = immovable; diarthrotic = freely movable.

| Functional class | Movement | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Synarthrosis** (synarthrotic) | **Immovable** | Sutures of the skull |
| **Amphiarthrosis** (amphiarthrotic) | **Slightly movable** (some **limited** degree of movement) | Intervertebral discs, symphysis pubis, distal tibiofibular joint |
| **Diarthrosis** (diarthrotic) | **Freely movable** | Most joints of the limbs (shoulder, elbow, hip, knee) |

> **Memory trick** (mnemonic)
>
> **S**yn = **S**tuck (no movement). **A**mphi = **A**bout half (a little movement). **D**i = **D**oes everything (free movement).

### ✅ Check yourself

The joint between the two pubic bones moves only slightly during childbirth. How is it classified functionally?

- A. Synarthrotic
- B. Amphiarthrotic
- C. Diarthrotic
- D. Synovial

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Amphiarthrotic**

Slightly movable joints are amphiarthroses. (Synovial is a structural term, not a functional one.)

</details>

## 8.3 Structural Classification: Fibrous Joints

`ch8-fibrous`

**Focus:**

- The parietal and temporal bones are joined by a **suture**.
- A suture is a **fibrous** joint (and functionally synarthrotic).

Structurally, joints are **fibrous**, **cartilaginous**, or **synovial**.

**Fibrous joints** are held together by dense **fibrous connective tissue**. They have **no joint cavity**. There are three types:

| Type | What holds the bones | Example | Movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Suture** | Very short collagen fibers | Between skull bones, such as the **parietal and temporal bones** (squamous suture) | Synarthrotic (immovable). Found **only in the skull** |
| **Syndesmosis** | A longer cord or sheet of fibrous tissue (a ligament) | **Distal tibiofibular joint**; acromioclavicular joint | Amphiarthrotic (a little "give") |
| **Gomphosis** | The short **periodontal ligament** | A **tooth in its socket** (alveolar process) | Synarthrotic |

## 8.4 Structural Classification: Cartilaginous Joints

`ch8-cartilaginous`

**Focus:**

- The joint between the bodies of the **vertebrae** and between the **two pubic bones** is a **symphysis**.
- The symphysis pubis is **not** a synovial joint and **not** a fibrous joint.

**Cartilaginous joints** are joined by **cartilage** and have **no joint cavity**. There are two types:

| Type | Cartilage | Examples | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Synchondrosis** | A plate of **hyaline cartilage** | **Epiphyseal plate**; the joint between the first rib and the manubrium | Temporary growth areas; usually synarthrotic. When ossified, the joint becomes a **synostosis** (a bony fusion) |
| **Symphysis** | A pad or plate of **fibrocartilage** | **Intervertebral discs** (between the bodies of adjacent vertebrae); **symphysis pubis** (between the two pubic bones) | Compressible shock absorber; **amphiarthrotic** |

> **Note** (note)
>
> The **symphysis pubis** is a **cartilaginous** joint, not a fibrous or synovial joint. So is every intervertebral disc.

> **Clinical connection** (clinical)
>
> **Epiphyseal plate versus intervertebral disc.** The epiphyseal plate is a **synchondrosis** of hyaline cartilage that is later replaced by bone. The intervertebral disc is a **symphysis** of fibrocartilage that stays cartilage for life.

## 8.5 Structure of a Synovial Joint

`ch8-synovial-structure`

**Focus:**

- Bones of a synovial joint are held together by a **joint capsule** (reinforced by ligaments).
- The **synovial membrane** secretes synovial fluid, surrounds the synovial cavity, and reabsorbs fluid.
- A **meniscus** is part of the **knee** joint.
- **Bursae** are fluid-filled sacs between skin and bony prominences.
- The articular ends of long bones are covered with **hyaline cartilage**.

**Synovial joints** have a **fluid-filled joint cavity** and are **freely movable (diarthroses)**. Most joints of the body are synovial, so **most types of joints are synovial**.

> 🖼 **Figure — Figure 8.A. General structure of a synovial joint, in section.** ([image](https://lsc-nh-biol2401-final-study-guide.netlify.app/public/figures/synovial-joint.webp))
> Description: A cutaway drawing of a synovial joint with one bone above and one below. The rounded end of each bone is covered by a blue-gray layer of articular cartilage. Between the two cartilages is the joint cavity, which contains synovial fluid (shown in pale yellow). Around the cavity is the articular capsule: an outer fibrous layer, and, lining its inside, the synovial membrane (shown in orange-red). Labels: bone (top and bottom), articular cartilage, synovial membrane, articular capsule, and joint cavity containing synovial fluid.

Features common to all synovial joints:

| Feature | Description and function |
|---|---|
| **Articular cartilage** | **Hyaline cartilage** covering the ends of the bones. It reduces friction and absorbs shock. It is the tissue that covers the articular ends of long bones. |
| **Joint capsule** | Double-layered sleeve around the joint. The **outer fibrous layer** is tough and continuous with the periosteum; it holds the bones together. |
| **Synovial membrane** | The **inner layer** of the capsule: loose connective tissue that lines the capsule and all internal surfaces except the articular cartilage. It **secretes synovial fluid** and **reabsorbs fluid**; its phagocytes can remove debris and microbes from an infected joint cavity. |
| **Synovial cavity** | The space between the bones; a "potential space" filled with synovial fluid. |
| **Synovial fluid** | Clear, viscous lubricant. **Reduces friction**, **nourishes cartilage**, and contains **phagocytes** that clean up debris. |
| **Ligaments** | Bands of dense connective tissue that connect **bone to bone** and **reinforce the joint capsule**. Many are thickened parts of the fibrous capsule. |

Other structures found in some joints:

- **Fatty pads** (hip and knee): cushion and fill space.
- **Menisci** (singular **meniscus**) or articular discs: C-shaped pads of **fibrocartilage** that divide the cavity into two compartments, absorb shock, and improve fit. **A meniscus is part of the knee joint** (medial and lateral menisci). Articular discs are also found in the jaw and the sternoclavicular joint.
- **Bursae** (singular bursa): flat fibrous sacs lined with synovial membrane and filled with synovial fluid, acting as **"ball bearings."** They lie between skin and bone, and between muscles, tendons, ligaments, and bone, where friction would occur. **Fluid-filled sacs between the skin and underlying bony prominences are bursae.**

**Three factors that influence joint stability:** 1. **Shape of the articular surfaces** (how well the bones fit). 2. **Ligaments** (number and strength). 3. **Muscle tone** (tendons and muscles crossing the joint).

> **Study tip** (tip)
>
> Remember the difference: **ligaments** connect **bone to bone**. **Tendons** connect **muscle to bone**. A joint capsule is reinforced by **ligaments**, not tendons.

### ✅ Check yourself

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

## 8.6 Types of Synovial Joints

`ch8-types`

**Focus:**

- **Rotational movement** is possible in the **ball-and-socket** joint (and pivot joints).
- The **ball-and-socket** joint allows the **most movements**.
- **Saddle** joint: base of the **thumb**. **Gliding** joint: between **carpals** (and tarsals). **Pivot**: atlas–axis.
- A **condyloid** joint does **not** allow rotation, and it is **not** limited to one plane.
- **Elbow and knee** are primarily **hinge** joints.

> 🖼 **Figure — Figure 8.B. The six types of synovial joints, with an example of each.** ([image](https://lsc-nh-biol2401-final-study-guide.netlify.app/public/figures/synovial-types.webp))
> Description: A skeleton in the center with six boxes marking joints, each linked to a simple model that shows the motion. (a) Pivot joint between the C1 and C2 vertebrae: a peg turning inside a ring. (b) Hinge joint at the elbow: a cylinder in a trough, moving in one plane. (c) Saddle joint between the trapezium carpal bone and the first metacarpal at the base of the thumb: two saddle-shaped surfaces. (d) Plane (gliding) joint between tarsal bones: flat surfaces sliding. (e) Condyloid joint between the radius and the carpal bones of the wrist: an oval head in an oval socket. (f) Ball-and-socket joint at the hip: a round head in a cup, moving in all directions.

| Type | Shape | Movements | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ball-and-socket joint** | Rounded head in a cup | **Most freely movable**: all angular movements **plus rotation** (flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, circumduction, rotation) | **Shoulder** and **hip** |
| **Condyloid joint** (ellipsoidal) | Oval head in oval cavity | All angular movements **except rotation**; can move in two planes | Wrist (radiocarpal), knuckles (metacarpophalangeal) |
| **Gliding joint** (plane) | Flat or slightly curved surfaces | Sliding or gliding back-and-forth | Between **carpals** and between **tarsals**; between vertebral processes; the **sternoclavicular** joint |
| **Hinge joint** | Cylinder in trough | **Flexion and extension only** (one plane) | **Elbow** (humerus–ulna), **knee**, interphalangeal joints |
| **Pivot joint** | Peg rotating in a ring | **Rotation** around a long axis | **Atlas–axis** (atlantoaxial joint; "no" movement); proximal radioulnar joint |
| **Saddle joint** | Two saddle-shaped surfaces | Wide range of movement, including **opposition** | **Base of the thumb** (carpometacarpal joint) |

> **Memory trick** (mnemonic)
>
> **"Ball and socket: the shoulder and hip rock."** **"Hinge: like a door at the elbow and knee."** **"Pivot: shake your head 'no.'"** **"Saddle: the thumb can touch every finger."**

### ✅ Check yourself

A dancer swings her leg in a wide circle and rotates it outward. Which joint type allows both?

- A. Hinge
- B. Condyloid
- C. Ball-and-socket
- D. Gliding

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Ball-and-socket**

Only ball-and-socket (and to a lesser extent pivot) joints permit rotation. The hip is a ball-and-socket joint that allows circumduction and rotation.

</details>

**Glossary terms in this section:** [Ball-and-socket joint](../glossary.md#ball-and-socket-joint), [Condyloid joint](../glossary.md#condyloid-joint), [Gliding joint](../glossary.md#gliding-joint), [Hinge joint](../glossary.md#hinge-joint), [Pivot joint](../glossary.md#pivot-joint), [Saddle joint](../glossary.md#saddle-joint)

## 8.7 Joint Movements

`ch8-movements`

**Focus:**

- **Dorsiflexion** brings the **foot closer to the shin**.
- **Adduction** means moving a body part **closer to the trunk (midline)**.

**Origin and insertion.** A muscle's **origin** is its attachment to the **relatively immovable** bone. Its **insertion** is its attachment to the **movable** bone. When a muscle contracts, the insertion is pulled toward the origin.

There are three general types of movement:

**1. Gliding movements.** Flat bone surfaces slide over one another (between carpals, between tarsals, between vertebrae).

**2. Angular movements** change the angle between bones:

| Movement | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Flexion** | **Decreases** the angle between two bones | Bending the elbow, nodding the head toward the chest |
| **Extension** | **Increases** the angle | Straightening the elbow |
| **Hyperextension** | Extension **beyond** the anatomical position | Tilting the head back |
| **Abduction** | Movement **away from the midline** | Raising the arm to the side |
| **Adduction** | Movement **toward the midline** | Lowering the arm to the side; bringing the thigh back toward the trunk |
| **Circumduction** | Moving a limb in a **cone-shaped circle** | Drawing a circle with your arm |
| **Rotation** | Turning of a bone **around its long axis** | Shaking your head "no" |
| **Dorsiflexion** | Bringing the **top of the foot closer to the shin** | Standing on your heels |
| **Plantar flexion** | **Pointing the toes** (flexion toward the sole) | Standing on tiptoe |

**3. Special movements**

| Movement | Description |
|---|---|
| **Supination** and **pronation** | Rotation of the forearm at the radioulnar joint: **palm up** (supination, "thumb out") and **palm down** (pronation) |
| **Inversion** and **eversion** | **Sole of the foot turned inward** (inversion) or **outward** (eversion) |
| **Elevation** and **depression** | **Lifting** a body part (shoulder shrug) and **lowering** it (opening the mouth depresses the mandible) |
| **Protraction** and **retraction** | **Forward** (thrust the jaw out) and **backward** (pull it back) |
| **Opposition** | Touching the thumb to the fingertips (saddle joint) |

> **Study tip** (tip)
>
> **"Adduction" = "add" to the body (toward the trunk). "Abduction" = "abduct" (take away) from the trunk.**

### ✅ Check yourself

- A. Plantar flexion
- B. Dorsiflexion
- C. Inversion
- D. Hyperextension of the knee

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Dorsiflexion**

Dorsiflexion decreases the angle between the top of the foot and the shin. Plantar flexion would point the toes.

</details>

## 8.8 Shoulder, Elbow, Hip, and Knee

`ch8-examples`

**Focus:**

- The anatomical term for the **shoulder joint** is the **glenohumeral joint**.
- The **rotator cuff** is found at the **shoulder**.
- The **radial collateral ligament** is associated with the **elbow**.
- The **shoulder** allows the most range of motion, so it is the **most likely to dislocate**.
- A **meniscus** is part of the **knee**.

| Joint | Type and bones | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| **Shoulder joint** (glenohumeral joint) | **Ball-and-socket**: head of the **humerus** in the shallow **glenoid cavity** of the scapula | **Greatest range of motion** of any joint, so it is the **most likely to dislocate**. Stabilized mainly by muscles and tendons: the **rotator cuff** (four muscles and their tendons) and a fibrous capsule. Many bursae. The acromioclavicular joint is a separate gliding/syndesmosis joint. |
| **Elbow joint** | **Hinge** between the **trochlea** of the humerus and the **trochlear notch** of the ulna (plus a gliding joint with the radius) | **Flexion and extension only**. Very stable because of strong ligaments, including the **radial collateral ligament** (lateral) and ulnar collateral ligament (medial). |
| **Hip joint** (coxal joint) | **Ball-and-socket**: head of the **femur** in the deep **acetabulum** | Strong and stable: deep socket plus large reinforcing ligaments. **Less range of motion than the shoulder** because of bony limits. |
| **Knee joint** | Largest and most complex synovial joint; **hinge** (with slight rotation), actually three joints: two **condyloid** (medial and lateral femur–tibia) and one **gliding** (patella–femur) | Flexion and extension. **Menisci** (medial and lateral), **ACL** and **PCL** (inside the capsule, prevent hyperextension and sliding), **tibial (medial)** and **fibular (lateral) collateral ligaments**, patellar ligament, many bursae. |

> 🖼 **Figure — Figure 8.C. The knee joint: sagittal section, superior view of the tibia, and anterior view.** ([image](https://lsc-nh-biol2401-final-study-guide.netlify.app/public/figures/knee-joint.webp))
> Description: Three views of the right knee. (a) A side section shows the femur above and the tibia below, the patella in front, the synovial cavity, the anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments, the lateral meniscus, the infrapatellar fat pad, and three bursae: suprapatellar, prepatellar, and infrapatellar. (b) A view from above of the tibia shows the C-shaped medial and lateral menisci and the crossing anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments. (c) A view from the front shows the quadriceps muscle and tendon, the patella and patellar ligament, the tibial (medial) and fibular (lateral) collateral ligaments, the tibia, and the fibula.

> 🖼 **Figure — Figure 8.D. The elbow joint: hinge between the humerus and ulna, with its collateral ligaments.** ([image](https://lsc-nh-biol2401-final-study-guide.netlify.app/public/figures/elbow-joint.webp))
> Description: Views of the right elbow showing the distal humerus meeting the ulna and radius. The ulna fits the trochlea of the humerus like a hinge, which allows only flexion and extension. The joint capsule is reinforced by the radial (lateral) collateral ligament and the ulnar (medial) collateral ligament, and the annular ligament holds the head of the radius against the ulna.

> **Memory trick** (mnemonic)
>
> **ACL** stops the tibia from sliding **A**nteriorly. **PCL** stops it from sliding **P**osteriorly. **MCL** is **M**edial and **LCL** is **L**ateral.

> **Note** (note)
>
> **Hip versus shoulder.** The hip allows **less** freedom of movement than the shoulder, because its deep socket and heavy ligaments make it more stable. The shoulder moves more but dislocates more easily. The **knee** (not the hip) is the largest and most complex synovial joint.

## 8.9 Life-Span Changes

`ch8-aging`

**Focus:**

- True statement: loss of function of synovial joints often begins in the **third decade of life**.

- The **fontanels** of the infant skull harden (ossify) within the **first two years**.
- **Epiphyseal plates** harden (close) between about **ages 14 and 20**.
- With age, **fibrocartilage loses water**, so intervertebral joints and knees become less flexible.
- **Collagen changes** cause stiffening beginning around **age 30**. Loss of function of synovial joints often begins in the **third decade of life** (in the twenties and thirties).
- **Exercise** delays the onset of joint stiffening.

### ✅ Check yourself

A 55-year-old complains that his knees and back feel stiffer each year, even though he has no injury. Which age-related change most likely contributes?

- A. Growth of the epiphyseal plates
- B. Fibrocartilage losing water and collagen stiffening
- C. Closure of the fontanels
- D. Loss of articular cartilage in infancy

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Fibrocartilage losing water and collagen stiffening**

With age, fibrocartilage dries and collagen stiffens, reducing flexibility. Regular exercise helps slow this process.

</details>

## 8.10 Joint Disorders: Injuries and Arthritis

`ch8-disorders`

**Focus:**

- A **dislocation** is displacement of the articulating bones, and the joint is clearly deformed.
- A **sprain** is overstretching or tearing of **ligaments and tendons** in a joint.
- **RA** is autoimmune; **OA** is caused by the effects of aging (wear and tear).
- **Lyme arthritis** is caused by a tick-borne bacterium.

**Joint injuries**

| Condition | Description |
|---|---|
| **Sprain** | **Overstretching or tearing of ligaments** (and sometimes tendons) in a joint |
| **Strain** | Overstretching or tearing of a **muscle** (for example, a hamstring injury) |
| **Dislocation** (luxation) | The **articulating bones are displaced** from their normal positions; the joint is clearly deformed. Often damages the capsule and ligaments |
| **Bursitis** | **Inflammation of a bursa** |
| **Tendinitis** | Inflammation of a tendon |
| **Ankylosis** | Abnormal stiffening or fusion of a joint |

**Arthritis** means **inflammation of the joints** (swollen, painful joints). Common forms:

| Type | Cause | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Osteoarthritis (OA)** | **Wear-and-tear / effects of aging**: articular cartilage softens and degenerates | Most common type; usually affects weight-bearing joints |
| **Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)** | **Autoimmune disease**: the immune system attacks the synovial membrane | Inflamed synovial membrane, joint deformity; can occur in younger adults and children (juvenile RA) |
| **Gout** (gouty arthritis) | **Uric acid crystals** deposit in the joint | Often affects the big toe |
| **Lyme arthritis** | **A bacterium** (*Borrelia burgdorferi*) **transmitted by a tick** bite | Joint swelling and pain, especially in the knees |

> **Study tip** (tip)
>
> **OA is "Old and Aching"** (aging). **RA is "Rogue Antibodies"** (autoimmune). They do **not** share the same cause.

### ✅ Check yourself

A hiker develops a swollen knee a few weeks after a tick bite and a bull's-eye rash. Which condition is most likely?

- A. Osteoarthritis
- B. Rheumatoid arthritis
- C. Gout
- D. Lyme arthritis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Lyme arthritis**

Lyme arthritis follows infection with *Borrelia burgdorferi* from a tick bite. OA is from aging, RA is autoimmune, and gout comes from uric acid crystals.

</details>

## 8.11 Joint Summary Table

`ch8-summary`

**Focus:**

- The **symphysis pubis** is a **cartilaginous** joint, **not** fibrous.
- The **hip** is **less** mobile than the **shoulder**, and the **knee** is the largest and most complex synovial joint.

| Joint | Structural class | Functional class | Bones | Movement | Based on movement |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Suture** | Fibrous | Synarthrotic | Skull bones | None | N/A |
| **Symphysis pubis** | Cartilaginous (symphysis) | Amphiarthrotic | Pubic bones of the coxae | Slight | Gliding / plane |
| **Intervertebral disc** | Cartilaginous (symphysis) | Amphiarthrotic | Adjacent vertebral bodies | Slight | Gliding |
| **Epiphyseal plate** | Cartilaginous (synchondrosis) | Synarthrotic | Epiphysis and diaphysis | None | N/A |
| **Distal tibiofibular** | Fibrous (syndesmosis) | Amphiarthrotic | Tibia, fibula | Slight | N/A |
| **Hip** | Synovial | Diarthrotic | Femur head, acetabulum | Flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, circumduction, rotation | Ball-and-socket |
| **Shoulder** | Synovial | Diarthrotic | Humerus head, glenoid cavity | Same as hip, with greater range | Ball-and-socket |
| **Elbow** | Synovial | Diarthrotic | Humerus, ulna | Flexion, extension | Hinge |
| **Knee** | Synovial | Diarthrotic | Femur, tibia, patella | Flexion, extension, slight rotation | Hinge (modified) |
| **Base of thumb** | Synovial | Diarthrotic | Trapezium, 1st metacarpal | Opposition and others | Saddle |
| **Atlas–axis** | Synovial | Diarthrotic | C1, C2 | Rotation | Pivot |

### ✅ Check yourself

Which pair of joints are both classified as amphiarthroses?

- A. Skull suture and shoulder
- B. Intervertebral disc and symphysis pubis
- C. Hip and knee
- D. Gomphosis and elbow

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Intervertebral disc and symphysis pubis**

Both discs and the pubic symphysis are fibrocartilage symphyses that allow slight movement.

</details>
