## 1.1 Structure, function, and levels of organization

`ch1-organization`

**Focus:**

- Connect a specific structural feature to its functional advantage.
- Trace the hierarchy in order; an organ contains multiple tissue types.

**Anatomy** examines structure; **physiology** explains function. Thin lung exchange surfaces are an anatomical feature that permits rapid gas diffusion, a physiological process. A structural change can therefore alter performance.

The course's expanded hierarchy is: subatomic particles → atoms → molecules → macromolecules → organelles → cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism. An atom retains an element's identity; a molecule contains joined atoms. Macromolecules such as proteins build cellular structures. Organelles perform specialized tasks inside cells. The **cell** is the basic living structural and functional unit. A tissue combines similar cells and extracellular material; an organ combines at least two tissue types; an organ system coordinates organs.

For example, contractile proteins form cellular machinery; cardiac muscle cells form cardiac muscle tissue; the heart combines muscle, nervous, connective, and epithelial tissues; the cardiovascular system includes the heart and vessels. Histology studies tissues, whereas cytology studies cells. Do not confuse an organelle such as a mitochondrion with an organ such as a kidney.

## 1.2 Life processes and survival requirements

`ch1-life`

**Focus:**

- Distinguish digestion, absorption, assimilation, and excretion.
- Match nutrients, oxygen, water, heat, and pressure to their contributions.

The ten course life processes describe different aspects of a living system. **Movement** changes position; **responsiveness** detects and responds to change; **growth** increases size or cell number; **reproduction** produces cells or a new organism. **Respiration** includes processes that make oxygen available and use it in cellular energy metabolism. **Digestion** breaks food into smaller components; **absorption** moves substances across a boundary into body fluids; **circulation** transports materials; **assimilation** incorporates absorbed substances into body structures; **excretion** removes metabolic wastes.

**Metabolism** is the total of chemical reactions, including synthesis and breakdown. Digestion, absorption, and assimilation are sequential but distinct: a protein is broken into amino acids, the amino acids cross the intestine, and cells use them to construct new proteins.

The five course requirements are nutrients, oxygen, water, suitable heat, and pressure. Nutrients supply energy and building materials. Oxygen accepts electrons during aerobic energy production. Water supports reactions and transport. Temperature affects enzymes; pressure gradients drive ventilation and filtration. Heat and pressure are physical conditions rather than chemical nutrients. Vital signs include body temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, and blood pressure; their interpretation depends on context.

## 1.3 Feedback and homeostasis

`ch1-homeostasis`

**Focus:**

- Negative feedback opposes a deviation; positive feedback amplifies a process.
- Trace temperature regulation through receptor, control center, and effectors.

**Homeostasis** maintains internal conditions within a useful range despite ongoing disturbances. It is a dynamic balance, not an absence of change. A variable such as temperature fluctuates around a regulated range; normal values vary with activity, time, and measurement site.

A receptor detects change, a control center integrates information relative to a reference range, and an effector produces a response. In **negative feedback**, the response opposes the initial deviation. When core temperature rises, hypothalamic circuits promote sweating and increased skin blood flow; evaporating sweat and heat transfer reduce temperature. Cooling can reduce sweating, constrict skin vessels, and trigger shivering, which generates heat. Name the stimulus, receptor, controller, effectors, and response when explaining a feedback loop.

**Positive feedback** amplifies an initiating process until a separate stopping event occurs. Platelet activation recruits additional platelets during clot formation; sealing the injury and regulatory mechanisms limit the process. Positive feedback is not synonymous with beneficial, nor negative feedback with harmful. Failure of homeostatic regulation can contribute to disease. Studying a feedback loop means explaining what the response does to the original stimulus, rather than merely memorizing the response's name.

## 1.4 Body cavities and serous membranes

`ch1-cavities`

**Focus:**

- Distinguish organ location from the potential space between serous layers.
- Visceral covers an organ; parietal lines a wall.

The axial body comprises the head, neck, and trunk; the appendicular portion comprises limbs. Dorsal cavities include the cranial cavity containing the brain and the vertebral canal containing the spinal cord. The diaphragm separates the thoracic cavity from the abdominopelvic cavity. The thorax includes two pleural compartments and the mediastinum; the heart lies within its pericardial sac in the mediastinum.

The abdominal portion contains much of the digestive tract, liver, gallbladder, and spleen; the pelvic portion includes the urinary bladder, rectum, and internal reproductive structures. No physical wall separates abdominal and pelvic portions. Kidneys, adrenal glands, and much of the pancreas are **retroperitoneal**, posterior to the peritoneal lining rather than suspended within the peritoneal cavity.

A **parietal** serous layer lines a cavity wall; a **visceral** layer covers an organ. Pleura relates to lungs, pericardium to the heart, and peritoneum to abdominopelvic organs. The thin fluid-filled potential space between layers permits low-friction movement. The lung itself is not located inside the thin pleural fluid space. Additional head spaces include oral, nasal, orbital, middle-ear cavities, and paranasal sinuses.

## 1.5 Eleven organ systems working together

`ch1-systems`

**Focus:**

- Associate each system with organs and a specific function.
- Bone marrow forms blood cells; kidneys regulate blood composition and produce urine.

| System | Representative organs | Main contributions |
|---|---|---|
| Integumentary | Skin, hair, nails, skin glands | Barrier, sensation, heat regulation, vitamin D precursor production |
| Skeletal | Bones, cartilage, ligaments | Support, protection, mineral storage, blood cell formation |
| Muscular | Skeletal muscles and tendons | Movement, posture, heat |
| Nervous | Brain, spinal cord, nerves | Rapid sensory processing and control |
| Endocrine | Hormone-secreting glands | Chemical regulation of growth, reproduction, and metabolism |
| Cardiovascular | Heart, blood vessels | Transport of gases, nutrients, hormones, and wastes |
| Lymphatic/immune | Lymph vessels, nodes, thymus, spleen | Fluid return, defense, transport of absorbed fats |
| Respiratory | Airways and lungs | Oxygen/carbon dioxide exchange and acid-base contribution |
| Digestive | Digestive tract, liver, pancreas, gallbladder | Food breakdown and nutrient absorption |
| Urinary | Kidneys, ureters, bladder, urethra | Waste excretion, fluid/electrolyte and acid-base regulation |
| Reproductive | Testes and ducts; ovaries, uterine tubes, uterus and associated structures | Gametes, sex hormones, reproduction |

Systems overlap functionally: skeletal marrow produces blood cells, while blood transports oxygen used by muscles. The kidneys form urine; the bladder stores it. Male and female reproductive structures belong to one system category in the eleven-system count. Organ function can depend on cooperation among multiple systems.

## 1.6 Anatomical position and directional terms

`ch1-directions`

**Focus:**

- Apply directional terms from the subject’s anatomical position.
- Proximal/distal refer to attachment, whereas medial/lateral refer to the midline.

In anatomical position, the person stands upright, faces forward, holds upper limbs at the sides, and turns palms anteriorly with forearms supinated. Right and left always refer to the subject's perspective. Directional descriptions retain this reference even if a person lies down.

| Pair | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Superior / inferior | Toward head / toward feet | The chest is superior to the pelvis. |
| Anterior / posterior | Toward front / toward back | The sternum is anterior to the heart. |
| Medial / lateral | Toward / away from midline | The nose is medial to the eyes. |
| Proximal / distal | Nearer / farther from attachment | The elbow is proximal to the wrist. |
| Superficial / deep | Nearer / farther from the surface | Skin is superficial to muscle. |
| Ipsilateral / contralateral | Same side / opposite sides | Right arm and right leg are ipsilateral. |

Ventral generally corresponds to anterior and dorsal to posterior for the human trunk. The thumb is lateral to the little finger in anatomical position. The subject's right may appear on the viewer's left. Use a comparison structure when applying a relational term rather than saying an isolated structure is simply proximal.

## 1.7 Body planes and surface landmarks

`ch1-planes`

**Focus:**

- Sagittal: right/left; coronal: front/back; transverse: upper/lower.
- Distinguish patellar from popliteal and brachial from antebrachial.

A sagittal plane divides right and left portions; midsagittal divides equal halves, whereas parasagittal divides unequal portions. A frontal or coronal plane divides anterior and posterior portions. A transverse or horizontal plane divides superior and inferior portions. Oblique sections cross at an angle. A cross-section is perpendicular to an object's long axis; a longitudinal section follows it.

Surface landmarks communicate location precisely. Cephalic means head; cranial skull; frontal forehead; orbital eye; otic ear; nasal nose; buccal cheek; oral mouth; mental chin; cervical neck; acromial shoulder; axillary armpit; brachial upper arm; antecubital anterior elbow; antebrachial forearm; carpal wrist; metacarpal hand; digital fingers or toes. Mammary refers to breast, umbilical navel, lumbar lower back, coxal hip, inguinal groin, pubic anterior pelvic region, gluteal buttock, femoral thigh, patellar front of knee, popliteal back of knee, crural leg, sural calf, tarsal ankle, pedal foot, plantar sole, and calcaneal heel.

The anatomical leg is the segment between knee and ankle, not the entire lower limb. Match a plane with the portions it produces rather than the direction in which a drawing is displayed.

## 1.8 Abdominal regions and quadrants

`ch1-regions`

**Focus:**

- Locate all nine regions and four quadrants using the subject’s right/left.
- Epigastric is superior to umbilical; hypogastric is inferior to it.

Two crossing reference lines create right upper, left upper, right lower, and left lower quadrants. The liver and gallbladder occupy much of the right upper quadrant; the stomach and spleen relate to the left upper quadrant. The appendix is typically in the right lower quadrant; sigmoid colon is associated with the left lower quadrant. Organs may extend across more than one region.

The nine-region arrangement uses two vertical and two horizontal reference lines. From the subject's right to left:

| Subject's right | Middle | Subject's left |
|---|---|---|
| Right hypochondriac: liver/gallbladder region | Epigastric: stomach and part of liver | Left hypochondriac: spleen/stomach |
| Right lumbar: ascending colon | Umbilical: small intestine/transverse colon | Left lumbar: descending colon |
| Right iliac/inguinal: cecum/appendix | Hypogastric/pubic: bladder and small intestine | Left iliac/inguinal: sigmoid colon |

The table follows the subject's right-to-left orientation; rows proceed from superior to inferior. **Epigastric** is upper middle; **hypogastric** is lower middle. A distended bladder rises from the pelvis toward the hypogastric region. Regional descriptions help locate findings but do not identify a diagnosis by themselves.
