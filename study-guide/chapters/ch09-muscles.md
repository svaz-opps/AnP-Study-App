## 9.1 Overview of Muscle Tissues

`ch9-overview`

There are three kinds of muscle tissue. All three have **elongated cells called muscle fibers**, all depend on two kinds of protein **myofilaments** (**actin** and **myosin**) to contract, and in all three the cell membrane is called the **sarcolemma** and the cytoplasm is called the **sarcoplasm**.

| Characteristic | **Skeletal** | **Smooth** | **Cardiac** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Location | Attached to bones | Walls of hollow organs and blood vessels; iris of the eye | Heart only |
| Cell shape | Long, cylindrical, **multinucleated** | Spindle-shaped, **one** nucleus | Branched, **one** nucleus (uninucleate) |
| Striations | **Yes** | **No** | **Yes** |
| Control | **Voluntary** | Involuntary | Involuntary |
| Contraction | Rapid, forceful, tires easily | Slow and sustained | Rhythmic and steady (**pacemaker**) |
| Special features | Organized into muscles | Peristalsis | **Intercalated discs** with gap junctions |

**Functions of muscle:** movement (walking, facial expression, blood pumping, digestion), **posture** maintenance, **joint stability**, and **heat generation** (almost half the energy released in contraction becomes heat).

**Four functional characteristics of muscle tissue**

| Characteristic | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Excitability** | Ability to receive and respond to a stimulus |
| **Contractility** | Ability to shorten forcibly |
| **Extensibility** | Ability to be stretched |
| **Elasticity** | Ability to recoil to the original length |

> **Memory trick** (mnemonic)
>
> **"E.C.E.E."**: **E**xcite, **C**ontract, **E**xtend, **E**lasticity bounce-back.

## 9.2 Structure of a Skeletal Muscle

`ch9-structure`

**Focus:**

- The **outermost layer** of connective tissue (underneath the fascia) surrounding a skeletal muscle is the **epimysium**.
- A **tendon attaches muscle to bone.**
- The severe pain of **compartment syndrome** results from **excessive fluid pressure** within the compartment.

Each skeletal muscle is an **organ** made of muscle fibers, connective tissue coverings, blood vessels, and nerves.

> 🖼 **Figure — Figure 9.A. Connective tissue coverings of a skeletal muscle.** ([image](https://lsc-nh-biol2401-final-study-guide.netlify.app/public/figures/muscle.webp))
> Description: The illustration shows a skeletal muscle attached to a bone by a tendon. A cutaway exposes bundles called fascicles, and one fascicle is pulled outward to reveal its individual muscle fibers. Epimysium surrounds the whole muscle, perimysium surrounds a fascicle, and endomysium surrounds each muscle fiber. A blood vessel is also labeled. The connective tissue coverings continue into the tendon at the end of the muscle. Deep fascia is discussed in the text but is not labeled in this illustration.

| Layer | What it wraps | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Endomysium** | **Each individual muscle fiber** | Thin and delicate |
| **Perimysium** | **Each fascicle** (bundle of fibers) | Collagen; carries blood vessels and nerves |
| **Epimysium** | **The entire muscle**: the **outermost** layer of connective tissue **just underneath the fascia** | Dense and fibrous |
| **Deep fascia** | Covers the muscle (and separates muscles) | Very tough outer layer that may extend as a tendon or aponeurosis |

- A **tendon** is a **cord of dense connective tissue that attaches muscle to bone**. (A **ligament** attaches bone to bone.)
- An **aponeurosis** is a **broad, flat sheet** of connective tissue that attaches a muscle to another muscle or to bone.
- A **raphe** is a seam where the fibers of two muscles meet.

> **Study tip** (tip)
>
> Order from inside out: **Endo**mysium (**in**side the fiber layer) → **Peri**mysium (**peri** = around the bundle) → **Epi**mysium (**epi** = on the outside).

> **Clinical connection** (clinical)
>
> **Compartment syndrome.** Groups of muscles are enclosed by fascia into compartments. After injury, swelling or bleeding inside the compartment raises **fluid pressure**, which squeezes blood vessels and nerves. The **severe pain** is caused by this **excessive fluid pressure within the compartment**, not by torn walls or stretched tendons. Untreated, it can cause permanent tissue damage; surgery (fasciotomy) relieves the pressure.

## 9.3 Skeletal Muscle Fiber and the Sarcomere

`ch9-fiber`

**Focus:**

- **Myofibrils** are composed primarily of **actin and myosin**.
- The **striated appearance** comes from the **sarcomere arrangement**.
- The **T tubules** transmit muscle impulses into the cell's interior; the **SR stores calcium**.
- The **sarcomere** is the **functional unit of contraction**.
- The **Z line** holds the bands of actin together and forms the **boundaries of the sarcomere**.

A muscle fiber is a long, thin cell packed with **myofibrils**. **Myofibrils are composed primarily of actin and myosin** (thin and thick filaments).

> 🖼 **Figure — Figure 9.B. A sarcomere, the functional unit of contraction.** ([image](https://lsc-nh-biol2401-final-study-guide.netlify.app/public/figures/sarcomere.webp))
> Description: The drawing shows one sarcomere between two Z lines. Thin actin filaments are attached to each Z line and extend toward the center. Thick myosin filaments sit in the middle. The A band is the dark region that spans the full length of the thick filaments. The I band is the light region containing only thin filaments, and it spans across the Z line into the next sarcomere. The H zone is the lighter central region of the A band with only thick filaments, and the M line is the center. When the muscle contracts, the Z lines move closer together, and the I band and H zone shorten while the A band keeps its length.

| Structure | Description |
|---|---|
| **Thick filament** | Made of the protein **myosin**: a rod-like tail with two globular heads (**cross-bridges**) |
| **Thin filament** | Made of **actin** (coiled like a twisted strand of pearls), plus **tropomyosin** (covers the binding sites) and **troponin** (binds calcium) |
| **A band** | **Dark** band: where thick and thin filaments **overlap** (the whole length of the thick filaments) |
| **I band** | **Light** band: **thin filaments only** |
| **Z line** | **Boundary between sarcomeres**: the structure that **holds the bands of actin together** at each end of the sarcomere |
| **Sarcomere** | The segment between two Z lines: **the functional (contractile) unit of the muscle** |

The **striations (stripes)** of skeletal muscle result from the **arrangement of thick and thin filaments in the sarcomeres** (alternating A and I bands).

**Special membranous organelles** - **Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR)**: network of membranous channels around each myofibril; **stores high concentrations of calcium ions** (maintained by a calcium pump) and releases them when stimulated. - **Transverse tubules (T tubules)**: inward extensions of the sarcolemma, filled with extracellular fluid, that **transmit the muscle impulse (action potential) deep into the cell's interior**. One T tubule sits between two enlarged ends of the SR (**terminal cisternae**), forming a **triad**.

> 🖼 **Figure — Muscle Fibes (large)** ([image](https://lsc-nh-biol2401-final-study-guide.netlify.app/public/figures/1007-muscle-fibes-large.webp))
> Description: Version 8.25 from the Textbook OpenStax Anatomy and Physiology Published May 18, 2016

## 9.4 The Neuromuscular Junction

`ch9-nmj`

**Focus:**

- **A motor unit** = a **motor neuron and the muscle fibers it controls**.
- **Neurotransmitters** are stored in **vesicles in the motor neuron endings**.
- **Cholinesterase** causes **acetylcholine to decompose**.
- A neuromuscular junction is a junction between a motor neuron and a muscle fiber, with a **gap (synaptic cleft)**, where **neurotransmitters are released** from the neuron: all of these statements are correct.
- The functional connection between neuron and fiber is the **synapse** (neuromuscular junction).

Skeletal muscle fibers contract only when stimulated by a **motor neuron**.

> 🖼 **Figure — Figure 9.C. The neuromuscular junction.** ([image](https://lsc-nh-biol2401-final-study-guide.netlify.app/public/figures/nmj.webp))
> Description: Three progressively enlarged views show the neuromuscular junction. At the top, a motor neuron's axon branches into terminals contacting a skeletal muscle fiber. The middle view enlarges one terminal and the motor end plate, separated by the synaptic cleft. At the bottom, synaptic vesicles release acetylcholine into the cleft by exocytosis. Acetylcholine binds receptors on the motor end plate, opening channels that allow sodium ions to enter the muscle fiber. Acetylcholinesterase and its role in ending the signal are explained in the text but are not labeled in this illustration.

- **Motor neuron**: the nerve cell that stimulates the muscle fiber.
- **Neuromuscular junction (NMJ)**: the **functional connection (synapse)** between a motor neuron's axon ending and a muscle fiber. It includes the **synaptic cleft**, a small gap, so **the neuron and muscle do not touch**.
- **Motor end plate**: the specialized part of the sarcolemma directly beneath the axon terminal, with receptors for acetylcholine.
- **Neurotransmitter**: chemical messenger released from the axon terminal. At the NMJ it is **acetylcholine (ACh)**. **Neurotransmitters are stored in vesicles within the motor neuron endings (axon terminals).**
- **Motor unit**: **one motor neuron and all the muscle fibers it controls.** Muscles that make fine movements (eye, fingers) have few fibers per neuron; powerful muscles have hundreds.
- **Acetylcholinesterase (AChE, also called cholinesterase)**: enzyme in the synaptic cleft that **breaks down (decomposes) acetylcholine**, so a single nerve impulse causes only one twitch.

> **Clinical connection** (clinical)
>
> **When the NMJ fails.** **Botulism** toxin blocks ACh release (flaccid paralysis). **Myasthenia gravis** is an autoimmune attack on ACh receptors (weakness). Some pesticides and nerve agents **inhibit acetylcholinesterase**, so ACh stays in the cleft and the muscle stays contracted (spastic paralysis).

### ✅ Check yourself

- A. ACh is never released
- B. ACh builds up in the cleft and the muscle stays stimulated
- C. Calcium stays in the sarcoplasmic reticulum
- D. ATP is no longer produced

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**ACh builds up in the cleft and the muscle stays stimulated**

Without the enzyme, ACh is not broken down, so the motor end plate is stimulated continuously and the muscle cannot relax.

</details>

## 9.5 Steps of Muscle Contraction

`ch9-contraction`

**Focus:**

- **The first event** in contraction: **acetylcholine is released from the end of the motor neuron.**
- **ATP supplies energy for myofilament movement.**
- Calcium released from the **SR** binds to **troponin**.

**The sequence (excitation–contraction coupling)**

1. A **motor impulse** from the brain travels down the spinal cord and along a motor neuron to the axon terminals.
2. **Acetylcholine is released** from the end of the motor neuron (calcium enters the terminal; vesicles release ACh by exocytosis). **This is the first event.**
3. ACh crosses the synaptic cleft and binds to receptors on the motor end plate, opening **ligand-gated channels** and **depolarizing** the sarcolemma.
4. The **muscle impulse travels over the surface and deep into the fiber through the T tubules**.
5. The impulse reaches the **sarcoplasmic reticulum**, which **releases calcium ions** into the sarcoplasm.
6. **Calcium binds to troponin**, which moves **tropomyosin** off the **myosin-binding sites** on actin.
7. **Cross-bridges form** between myosin heads and actin.
8. The myosin heads **pivot (power stroke)**, pulling the thin filaments toward the center of the sarcomere. **ATP supplies the energy for this myofilament movement.** The fiber shortens.

**Cross-bridge cycle.** (a) ATP is split to "cock" the myosin head; (b) the cocked head attaches to actin; (c) the head pivots (power stroke) and pulls the actin; (d) a **new ATP binds** and the head releases from actin. The cycle repeats as long as calcium and ATP are present.

**Sliding filament model** (Huxley, 1954): thin filaments slide past thick filaments. The filaments themselves do not shorten.

**Relaxation** 1. **Acetylcholinesterase** destroys ACh, ending stimulation. 2. Calcium is **pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum** using ATP. 3. Tropomyosin covers the binding sites, cross-bridges break, and the fiber relaxes.

> **Memory trick** (mnemonic)
>
> **"A-T-C-C-S":** **A**Ch → **T** tubule → **C**alcium released → **C**ross-bridges → **S**lide.

> **Clinical connection** (clinical)
>
> **Rigor mortis.** After death, ATP is no longer made. Without ATP, myosin heads cannot detach from actin, so the muscles stay stiff until the proteins break down.

## 9.6 Energy for Contraction

`ch9-energy`

**Focus:**

- **Creatine phosphate** supplies energy for the **synthesis of ATP**.
- The oxygen needed to convert **lactic acid back to glycogen** after intense exercise is the **oxygen debt**.
- A **muscle cramp** is most likely due to a lack of **ATP**.

Muscle stores only about **six seconds** of ATP, so ATP must be regenerated continuously. There are **three pathways**:

| Pathway | How it makes ATP | Duration and notes |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Creatine phosphate (CP)** | **CP + ADP → creatine + ATP** | About 10 seconds. CP **supplies energy for the synthesis of ATP** (it is not itself used to break ATP down) |
| **2. Anaerobic respiration** (glycolysis) | Glucose → pyruvic acid + **2 ATP** in the cytoplasm; no oxygen needed | Fast but low yield; if oxygen is short, pyruvic acid becomes **lactic acid** |
| **3. Aerobic respiration** | Citric acid cycle and electron transport chain in the **mitochondria**; needs oxygen | About **36 ATP** per glucose plus CO₂ and water; sustained, long-term |

**Oxygen debt** (excess post-exercise oxygen consumption): the **amount of oxygen needed after intense exercise to support the conversion of lactic acid back to glucose/glycogen**, resynthesize ATP and CP, and restore oxygen levels in blood and tissues. This is why you keep breathing hard after you stop.

**Muscle fatigue** is the inability to contract, caused by a relative deficit of ATP and/or **lactic acid accumulation** (which lowers pH).

**Muscle cramp.** A cramp is most likely due to a **lack of ATP**: without ATP, calcium cannot be pumped back into the SR and myosin heads cannot detach, so the muscle stays contracted.

**Heat.** Almost half the energy released in contraction is lost as heat, which helps keep body temperature near 37 °C. Excess heat is lost through negative feedback mechanisms such as sweating and dilation of skin blood vessels.

## 9.7 Muscle Responses

`ch9-responses`

**Focus:**

- **Latent period** = delay between stimulus and response. **Refractory period** = brief unresponsive moment after stimulation.
- **Threshold stimulus** = the **minimal** stimulus needed to elicit a contraction.
- Matching: **isotonic** = movement where fibers lengthen or shorten; **eccentric** = lengthening contraction; **concentric** = shortening contraction; **isometric** = tension rises, length unchanged; **recruitment** = more motor units; **twitch** = response of one fiber to one impulse; **tone** = sustained contraction.
- Red muscles contract more **slowly**, have **more** myoglobin and mitochondria, and fatigue more **slowly**.

**Threshold stimulus**: the **minimal stimulus needed to elicit a muscular contraction**. In a muscle fiber the membrane potential must change from about **−100 mV to −70 mV (a +30 mV change)** for an impulse to begin.

**All-or-none response**: a muscle fiber that receives a stimulus at or above threshold contracts **completely**; a subthreshold stimulus produces **no** contraction.

**Recording a twitch (a myogram)**

> 🖼 **Figure — Figure 9.D. A myogram of a single muscle twitch.** ([image](https://lsc-nh-biol2401-final-study-guide.netlify.app/public/figures/twitch.webp))
> Description: The myogram plots muscle tension on the vertical axis against time in milliseconds on the horizontal axis. A single stimulus occurs at time zero. A short latent period precedes the rise in tension. Tension then rises during the contraction period and falls toward baseline during the relaxation period. The three periods have different background shading and are also labeled in words. The refractory period is discussed in the text but is not separately marked on this graph.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Twitch** | A **single, brief contraction** (a fraction of a second) in response to one impulse |
| **Latent period** | The **delay between the stimulus and the start of the response** |
| **Refractory period** | The **very brief moment after stimulation when the fiber is unresponsive** to another stimulus |
| **Staircase effect (treppe)** | A rested muscle gives a series of twitches, each slightly stronger than the last |
| **Summation** | Twitches combine when stimuli arrive faster than the fiber can relax, producing stronger, sustained contraction |
| **Tetanus** (tetanic contraction) | Sustained contraction with **no relaxation** between stimuli |
| **Recruitment** | **Increase in the number of activated motor units** as stimulus intensity rises |
| **Muscle tone** | A **certain degree of sustained contraction** in a resting muscle; maintains posture |

**Types of contraction** - **Isotonic contraction**: the muscle **changes length** (and the load moves). **Concentric** = shortening contraction; **eccentric** = lengthening contraction. - **Isometric contraction**: **tension increases but the length does not change** (pushing against a wall).

**Fast versus slow muscle fibers**

| Characteristic | **Slow-twitch (red)** | **Fast-twitch (white)** |
|---|---|---|
| Contraction speed | **Slower** | **Faster** |
| Myoglobin | **More** (red color) | Less |
| Mitochondria | **More** | **Fewer** |
| Blood supply | Rich | Less |
| Fatigue | **Slowly** (endurance) | Quickly (lactic acid) |
| Example | Postural muscles, marathon runners | Eye, sprinters; "white meat" |

> **Note** (note)
>
> **Watch for the false comparison:** red muscles do **not** have fewer mitochondria than white muscles. They have **more**.

## 9.8 Atrophy, Hypertrophy, and Muscle Disorders

`ch9-disorders`

**Focus:**

- Muscle atrophy from disuse: reduced capillaries, fewer mitochondria, and smaller actin and myosin filaments: **all of these**.

- **Hypertrophy**: enlargement of muscle fibers from heavy use (exercise).
- **Atrophy**: **wasting** of muscle in response to **disuse**. Atrophy reduces the **capillary networks**, the number of **mitochondria**, and the size of **actin and myosin filaments**. So **all of these are correct**.
- **Tendinitis**: inflammation of a tendon.
- **Muscle strain**: overstretching or tearing of muscle fibers (a pulled hamstring).
- **Compartment syndrome**: pressure buildup in a muscle compartment (see Structure).
- **Duchenne muscular dystrophy**: inherited disease with progressive muscle degeneration.
- **Poliomyelitis**: virus damages motor neurons.
- **Botulism**, **myasthenia gravis**, **rigor mortis**: see neuromuscular junction and contraction.
- **TMJ syndrome**: pain in the jaw joint and chewing muscles.
- **Parkinson's disease**: tremors and rigidity from loss of dopamine neurons.

**Life-span changes.** ATP, myoglobin, and creatine phosphate supplies start to decline in the **forties**. By age 80, about **half of muscle mass** has been replaced by connective and adipose tissue. **Exercise is the best way to maintain muscle function.**

**Disuse caused atrophy: fewer mitochondria, less capillary supply, and smaller myofilaments**

Without use, muscle fibers shrink: myofilaments get smaller, mitochondria decrease, and capillary networks decline.

</details>

## 9.9 Smooth and Cardiac Muscle

`ch9-smooth-cardiac`

**Smooth muscle** - No striations; lacks organized sarcomeres. **No troponin**: **calmodulin** binds calcium instead. Little SR; most calcium enters from **extracellular fluid**. - **Multiunit smooth muscle**: fibers act independently; rapid, vigorous contractions. **Examples: iris of the eye, walls of large blood vessels.** - **Visceral (single-unit) smooth muscle**: fibers linked by gap junctions, contracting as a sheet; **slow and sustained**, rhythmic. Found in the walls of **hollow organs** (stomach, intestine, uterus, bladder). Arranged in an **inner circular** and **outer longitudinal** layer. - **Peristalsis**: wave-like contractions that **push substances through tubes** (food through the intestine). - Neurotransmitters: acetylcholine and norepinephrine.

**Cardiac muscle** - **Only in the heart.** Striated, **branched** fibers, **one nucleus** each. - Joined end to end by **intercalated discs** with **gap junctions** so the heart works as a **syncytium**. - **Self-exciting** (pacemaker), rhythmic, **involuntary** (about 60–100 beats per minute). - Less developed SR and many mitochondria.

Which tissue is striated, involuntary, and has intercalated discs?

- A. Skeletal muscle
- B. Smooth muscle
- C. Cardiac muscle
- D. Visceral smooth muscle

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Cardiac muscle**

Cardiac muscle is striated and involuntary with intercalated discs. Skeletal muscle is voluntary, and smooth muscle lacks striations.

</details>

## 9.10 How Muscles Produce Movement

`ch9-actions`

**Focus:**

- A muscle end attached to a relatively immovable part is the **origin**.
- The **antagonist** opposes the movement of the prime mover (agonist).

Muscles pull on bones by **tendons** to move them as **levers**. Every skeletal muscle has two attachments:

- **Origin**: the attachment to the **relatively immovable** (stationary) bone.
- **Insertion**: the attachment to the **movable** bone. On contraction, the insertion is pulled toward the origin.

**How muscles cooperate**

| Role | Meaning | Example (elbow flexion) |
|---|---|---|
| **Prime mover** (agonist) | The muscle **mainly responsible** for the movement | **Biceps brachii** |
| **Synergist** | Assists the prime mover | **Brachialis**, brachioradialis |
| **Antagonist** | **Opposes** the movement; it relaxes or stretches while the prime mover contracts | **Triceps brachii** |
| **Fixator** | Stabilizes the origin of the prime mover | Serratus anterior and pectoralis minor hold the scapula |

Another example: in the thigh, the **quadriceps** (extend the knee) are antagonists of the **hamstrings** (flex the knee).

> **Study tip** (tip)
>
> The general name for a muscle that **opposes** a movement is the **antagonist**. Do not confuse it with agonist (prime mover).

### ✅ Check yourself

When you straighten your elbow, the triceps brachii contracts. What is the biceps brachii called in this movement?

- A. Prime mover
- B. Fixator
- C. Synergist
- D. Antagonist

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Antagonist**

The biceps opposes elbow extension, so it is the antagonist while the triceps is the prime mover.

</details>

## 9.11 Naming Skeletal Muscles

`ch9-naming`

Muscle names often describe one of these features:

| Naming characteristic | Terms | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Direction of fibers** | rectus = straight/parallel; transverse = across; oblique = angled | Rectus abdominis, transversus abdominis, external oblique |
| **Location** | named for a bone or region | **Frontalis** (over the frontal bone), tibialis anterior, temporalis |
| **Size** | maximus (largest), longus (long), brevis (short) | Gluteus maximus, palmaris longus |
| **Number of heads** | bi- = 2, tri- = 3, quad- = 4 | Biceps brachii, triceps brachii, quadriceps |
| **Shape** | deltoid = triangle, trapezius = trapezoid, serratus = saw-toothed, orbicularis = circular | Deltoid, trapezius, serratus anterior, orbicularis oris |
| **Origin and insertion** | uses the attachment sites | **Sternocleidomastoid** (sternum and clavicle → mastoid process) |
| **Action** | flexor, extensor, adductor, abductor | Flexor carpi radialis, extensor digitorum, adductor longus |

### ✅ Check yourself

A muscle named "extensor digitorum longus" is described. What can you infer?

- A. It has three heads
- B. It is a triangular shoulder muscle
- C. It extends the digits and is relatively long
- D. It runs diagonally across the abdomen

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**It extends the digits and is relatively long**

"Extensor" names the action (extends), "digitorum" the location (digits), and "longus" the size (long).

</details>

## 9.12 Muscles of the Head and Neck

`ch9-head`

**Focus:**

- The muscle used to **raise the eyebrows** is the **frontalis**.

| Muscle | Location | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Frontalis** (part of the epicranius) | Over the **forehead** | **Raises the eyebrows** |
| **Occipitalis** (part of the epicranius) | Over the back of the skull | Pulls the scalp backward |
| **Orbicularis oculi** | Circular, around the **eye** | Closes the eye (blinking, squinting) |
| **Orbicularis oris** | Circular, around the **mouth** | Closes and purses the lips ("kissing muscle") |
| **Zygomaticus** (*) | Zygomatic arch to the corner of the mouth | Raises the corners of the mouth ("smiling muscle") |
| **Buccinator** | Cheek | Compresses the cheek ("trumpeter's muscle") |
| **Platysma** | Over the neck and lower jaw | Depresses the lower jaw / tenses the neck skin |
| **Masseter** (*) | Origin: **zygomatic arch**; insertion: **lateral mandible** | **Elevates the mandible** (chewing) |
| **Temporalis** | Over the temporal bone | Elevates the mandible |
| **Sternocleidomastoid** (*) | Origin: sternum and clavicle; insertion: **mastoid process** | Flexes the head toward the chest (both sides); rotates the head (one side) |
| **Erector spinae** | Along the vertebral column | Extends the spine; maintains **posture** |

> **Study tip** (tip)
>
> Muscles marked (*) are the ones for which the outline asks you to know the **origin and insertion**.

## 9.13 Muscles of the Shoulder and Arm

`ch9-arm`

**Focus:**

- The muscle used for **abduction of the arm** is the **deltoid**.

| Muscle | Location / origin and insertion | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Trapezius** (*) | Origin: occipital bone and spines of C7–T12; insertion: clavicle and acromion/scapular spine | **Elevates** the pectoral girdle ("shoulder shrug"); adducts the scapula |
| **Serratus anterior** | Saw-toothed muscle on the lateral thorax | **Fixator** of the scapula; protracts the scapula |
| **Pectoralis minor** | Deep to pectoralis major | **Fixator** of the scapula |
| **Pectoralis major** (*) | Origin: clavicle, sternum, costal cartilages of ribs 1–6; insertion: **greater tubercle of humerus** | **Flexes and adducts** the arm (pulls the arms forward and together) |

| **Deltoid** | Triangular shoulder muscle | **Abducts** the arm (also flexes and extends) |

| **Brachialis** | Beneath the biceps | Flexes the elbow (synergist) |
| **Brachioradialis** | Lateral forearm | Flexes the elbow (synergist) |
| **Triceps brachii** | Posterior arm, **three heads** | **Extends** the elbow (antagonist of the biceps) |
| **Flexor carpi radialis / ulnaris, palmaris longus** | Anterior forearm | **Flex the wrist** |
| **Extensor digitorum** | Posterior forearm | **Extends the wrist and fingers** |

## 9.15 Muscles of the Hip, Thigh, and Leg

`ch9-leg`

**Focus:**

- The group of muscles used for **flexing the lower limb at the knee** is the **hamstrings** (not the quadriceps).

| Muscle | Location | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Gluteus maximus** (*) | Buttock; **largest muscle in the body**; origin: dorsal ilium, sacrum, coccyx | **Extends the thigh** (climbing stairs, standing up) |
| **Gluteus medius** | Lateral hip | **Abducts** the thigh |
| **Adductor longus** | Medial thigh | **Adducts** the thigh |
| **Quadriceps femoris**: rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, vastus intermedius | **Anterior** thigh | **Extend the leg at the knee** |
| **Sartorius** (*) | Longest strap-like muscle crossing the thigh; origin: iliac spine; insertion: medial tibia | Flexes the thigh and knee ("tailor's muscle") |
| **Hamstrings**: biceps femoris, semitendinosus, semimembranosus | **Posterior** thigh | **Flex the leg at the knee** (and extend the hip) |
| **Tibialis anterior** | Anterior to the tibia | **Dorsiflexion** |
| **Fibularis (peroneus) longus** | Lateral to the fibula | **Eversion** |
| **Gastrocnemius** (*) | Calf; origin: femoral condyles; insertion: calcaneus (**calcaneal / Achilles tendon**) | **Plantar flexion** (prime mover) |
| **Soleus** | Deep to the gastrocnemius | Plantar flexion (synergist) |

> **Memory trick** (mnemonic)
>
> **Quads = "quad" (four) heads extend the knee. Hamstrings bend ("flex") the knee** (the ham in the back of the thigh).
