## 10.1 Nervous-system organization and information flow

`ch10-divisions`

**Focus:**

- CNS is brain/spinal cord; PNS includes peripheral nerves and ganglia.
- Afferent arrives, efferent exits; integration links input to output.

The central nervous system (CNS) consists of brain and spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system (PNS) includes nerves, ganglia, and peripheral receptors outside the CNS. Sensory or afferent pathways carry information toward the CNS; motor or efferent pathways carry commands away. Interneurons connect and integrate within central circuits.

Sensory input detects changes; integration interprets and selects responses; motor output activates effectors. Somatic motor pathways supply skeletal muscle, whereas autonomic motor pathways supply smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands. Visceral describes internal-organ relationships; somatic describes body-wall/limb relationships. Sensory traffic can also be visceral or somatic, so visceral is not simply another word for every motor axon.

A receptor converts a stimulus into a signal. An effector performs a response. For example, skin stimulation activates sensory neurons, spinal circuits process input, and somatic motor neurons activate skeletal muscle. A reflex can be integrated in the spinal cord before conscious awareness. Directional labels are defined relative to the CNS, not whether a particular line in a diagram points left or right.

## 10.2 Neuron structure and classification

`ch10-neurons`

**Focus:**

- Locate receptive regions, trigger zone, conducting axon, and secretory terminal.
- Distinguish structural neuron classes from sensory/motor/interneuron roles.

A neuron has a cell body or soma, dendrites, and an axon that can give off collaterals and end in axon terminals/synaptic knobs. Dendrites and soma commonly receive input; the axon initial segment commonly initiates action potentials; the axon carries them to terminals. Nissl substance consists of rough ER/ribosomal material in the soma and dendrites, supporting protein synthesis. Mature neurons have limited cell-cycle reentry; simplistic claims that one missing organelle alone explains all inability to divide are incomplete.

Multipolar neurons have one axon and multiple dendrites and are common among CNS interneurons and somatic motor neurons. Bipolar neurons have one main dendritic and one axonal process, as in selected special-sensory pathways. Pseudounipolar sensory neurons have one process that splits into peripheral and central branches, with cell bodies in sensory ganglia.

Functional classification asks where information goes; structural classification asks about processes. They are related but not interchangeable. A damaged axon may regenerate under favorable PNS conditions if its cell body survives; extensive cell-body injury is more likely to destroy the neuron. CNS regeneration is far more restricted. Axonal transport depends on cytoskeletal tracks and moves organelles/materials between soma and terminals.

## 10.3 Glia, myelin, gray matter, and white matter

`ch10-glia`

**Focus:**

- Compare Schwann cells with oligodendrocytes and distinguish the four CNS glial types.
- Myelin explains white matter and supports saltatory conduction.

CNS astrocytes regulate extracellular conditions and support the blood-brain barrier; the barrier's restrictive seal primarily depends on specialized endothelial tight junctions. Oligodendrocytes myelinate segments of multiple CNS axons. Microglia provide immune surveillance and phagocytic functions. Ependymal cells line ventricles and the spinal central canal; specialized associated cells participate in cerebrospinal fluid production at choroid plexuses.

In the PNS, Schwann cells support axons and each forms one myelin segment around one axon. Satellite cells surround cell bodies in peripheral ganglia. Small unmyelinated axons can still be supported within Schwann cell grooves without a thick multilayer myelin sheath. Nodes of Ranvier are gaps between myelin segments where ion channels support action potential regeneration.

White matter contains many myelinated axons; gray matter contains neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, synapses, and less-myelinated processes. Location differs: spinal gray matter is central, while cerebral cortical gray matter lies superficially with deeper gray nuclei. Myelin increases electrical insulation and speeds conduction; damage disrupts signaling. Glia are active regulators rather than merely passive packing material.

## 10.4 Resting potential and graded signals

`ch10-resting`

**Focus:**

- Distinguish maintained ion gradients from moment-to-moment channel currents.
- Depolarization approaches zero; hyperpolarization becomes more negative.

Membrane potential is voltage across a cell membrane. A typical resting neuron is about −70 mV inside relative to outside, although values vary. Potassium is relatively concentrated inside and sodium outside. Selective permeability, especially potassium leak conductance, produces much of the resting voltage. The sodium–potassium pump maintains concentration gradients by moving three Na+ outward and two K+ inward per ATP.

Depolarization makes the membrane less negative; hyperpolarization makes it more negative. Local graded potentials vary in size, can decay with distance, and may be excitatory or inhibitory. Excitatory postsynaptic potentials often bring a trigger region closer to threshold; inhibitory effects can oppose firing. A subthreshold depolarization can facilitate a later response without itself becoming an action potential.

Temporal summation combines closely spaced signals from one input; spatial summation combines inputs at different locations. Whether threshold is reached depends on their integrated effect at the trigger region. The pump does not suddenly create each action potential on its own, and a resting neuron is not electrically uncharged. Voltage is a difference in charge distribution across a very thin membrane boundary.

## 10.5 Action potentials and conduction

`ch10-impulse`

**Focus:**

- Sodium influx depolarizes; potassium efflux repolarizes.
- Predict conduction speed from myelination and diameter.

When a trigger region reaches threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels open rapidly and sodium enters, driving depolarization. Sodium channels then inactivate while voltage-gated potassium channels open more slowly; potassium leaves, producing repolarization and often a brief after-hyperpolarization. Channels recover as membrane voltage returns toward resting conditions. Exact threshold and peak values vary among neurons.

An action potential is all-or-none once initiated in a healthy axon segment; greater stimulus strength is usually represented by firing frequency and recruited neurons rather than a taller action potential in the same axon. The absolute refractory period prevents another action potential during sodium-channel unavailability; the relative refractory period requires a stronger stimulus.

Local currents depolarize adjacent membrane, regenerating the signal along an unmyelinated axon. In a myelinated axon, current spreads rapidly beneath myelin and action potentials regenerate at nodes: saltatory conduction. Large diameter reduces internal resistance and generally increases speed; myelination also increases speed. The action potential normally travels from trigger zone to terminals because preceding membrane is refractory, not because sodium ions themselves race the entire axon length.

## 10.6 Chemical synapses and neurotransmitter effects

`ch10-synapses`

**Focus:**

- Trace calcium-triggered exocytosis and receptor-dependent postsynaptic response.
- Explain transmitter removal and distinguish convergence from divergence.

At a chemical synapse, an arriving action potential opens voltage-gated calcium channels in the presynaptic terminal. Calcium entry triggers vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter exocytosis. Transmitter diffuses across the cleft, binds postsynaptic receptors, and changes channel or signaling activity. It does not cross as an intact action potential. Directionality follows polarized release and receptor machinery.

Acetylcholine acts at neuromuscular junctions and many autonomic synapses. Glutamate is a major excitatory CNS transmitter; GABA is a major inhibitory one. Glycine commonly mediates spinal inhibition. Dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin modulate selected pathways. Effects depend on receptor subtype and target, so a transmitter is not universally excitatory in every tissue. Endorphins and enkephalins are neuropeptides involved in pain modulation.

Signals end through enzymatic breakdown, reuptake, or diffusion. Acetylcholinesterase breaks down acetylcholine; reuptake transporters remove many monoamines. Drugs can alter synthesis, release, breakdown, transporters, or receptors. Disorders can involve transmitter pathways, but statements that one transmitter alone fully explains every case are oversimplifications. Convergence brings several inputs to one neuron; divergence distributes one input to several targets. Neuronal pools integrate information through interconnected circuits.
