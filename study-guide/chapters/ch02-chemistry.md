## 2.1 Elements, atoms, isotopes, and electrons

`ch2-atoms`

**Focus:**

- Calculate protons, neutrons, and electrons separately.
- Distinguish isotope, ion, mass number, and average atomic mass.

Matter occupies space and has mass. An element consists of atoms with the same proton number. A proton is positively charged and a neutron uncharged; both occupy the nucleus and contribute approximately one atomic mass unit. Electrons have negative charge, occupy an electron cloud, and have much less mass, not zero mass.

Atomic number equals proton number. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. Mass number counts protons plus neutrons for one isotope; periodic-table atomic mass is an abundance-weighted average of isotopes. Isotopes share proton numbers but differ in neutron numbers. Changing electrons makes an ion; changing neutrons makes another isotope of the same element.

In the simplified shell model used here, the first shell holds up to two electrons and the next holds up to eight for the common light elements. Nitrogen has seven electrons in a neutral atom: two inner and five outer electrons. Outer **valence electrons** strongly influence bonding. Oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, calcium, and phosphorus are major body elements. Other important symbols include Na sodium, K potassium, Cl chlorine, Mg magnesium, S sulfur, and Fe iron. Do not confuse the sodium symbol Na with nitrogen N.

## 2.2 Ionic, covalent, and hydrogen bonding

`ch2-bonds`

**Focus:**

- Electron transfer produces ions; sharing produces covalent bonds.
- Distinguish bonds within water from hydrogen bonds between water molecules.

Ionic bonding follows electron transfer and electrostatic attraction. When sodium loses an electron it becomes Na+, a cation; chlorine gains an electron and becomes Cl−, an anion. Neither changes its proton number. Opposite charges attract. Dissolving sodium chloride in water separates ions that can carry current.

Covalent bonds share electron pairs. Nonpolar bonds share relatively equally; polar covalent bonds share unequally. Water contains polar O–H covalent bonds, with partial negative charge near oxygen and partial positive charge near hydrogen. A molecule can contain polar bonds yet have no overall dipole if its geometry cancels the polarities.

Hydrogen bonds are attractions involving a partially positive hydrogen and an electronegative partner. They help organize liquid water, stabilize protein shapes, and connect complementary DNA bases. They are weaker individually than covalent bonds. The bonds within a water molecule are covalent; attractions between water molecules include hydrogen bonds. A compound contains atoms of at least two different elements; O2 is a molecule but not a compound. In aqueous biological conditions, compare bonds in context: dissolved ions behave differently from ions in an intact crystal.

## 2.3 Chemical reactions and energy

`ch2-reactions`

**Focus:**

- Recognize synthesis, decomposition, exchange, and reversible equations.
- Hydrolysis consumes water; dehydration synthesis releases water.

Synthesis combines smaller units: A + B → AB. Decomposition separates a larger compound: AB → A + B. Exchange rearranges partners: AB + CD → AD + CB. Reversible reactions proceed in either direction depending on conditions, represented with a double arrow. At equilibrium, forward and reverse rates balance; molecules still react.

Anabolism builds larger biological molecules and requires energy input overall. Catabolism breaks down molecules and often releases usable energy overall. Dehydration synthesis joins subunits while removing the components of water; hydrolysis uses water to break a linkage. Building a peptide from amino acids and digesting that peptide illustrate opposite directions.

Energy is needed to break chemical bonds, while bond formation releases energy. Whether the whole reaction releases or requires energy depends on the balance between reactants and products. A catalyst lowers activation energy and speeds a reaction without being consumed; it does not change the overall energy difference or equilibrium position. Temperature, concentration, surface area, and catalyst availability affect reaction rates. Enzymes catalyze particular biological steps rather than making every possible reaction happen indiscriminately.

## 2.4 Water, salts, and inorganic substances

`ch2-water`

**Focus:**

- Explain water’s solvent, transport, reaction, and thermal roles.
- A dissolved substance conducts current only when mobile charged particles are available.

Water is the most abundant inorganic substance in many cells. Its polarity makes it an effective solvent for ions and many polar compounds. Water also provides a reaction medium, participates in hydrolysis, lubricates tissues, cushions structures, and transports dissolved materials. Its relatively high heat capacity limits rapid temperature change; evaporation removes substantial heat.

Hydrogen bonding contributes to water's cohesion and thermal properties. Oil does not mix well with water because most lipid regions are nonpolar. Hydrophilic substances interact with water; hydrophobic regions avoid favorable mixing with it. Water is not a universal solvent for every substance.

Inorganic substances important to physiology include water, oxygen, carbon dioxide, and salts. Oxygen participates in aerobic cellular respiration; carbon dioxide is a metabolic product and contributes to acid-base chemistry. Salts supply ions such as sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride, phosphate, and bicarbonate. These support electrical signaling, contraction, fluid balance, mineralized tissue, and buffering. Dissociation of an electrolyte produces ions in solution, whereas dissolving a nonelectrolyte such as glucose need not produce ions. Concentration of dissolved particles influences osmosis, but charge and membrane permeability also matter. Organic biological compounds generally have carbon frameworks and often carbon–hydrogen bonds. Carbon dioxide and bicarbonate are important carbon-containing compounds still classified as inorganic. The four major organic classes studied here are carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids.

## 2.5 Acids, bases, pH, and buffers

`ch2-ph`

**Focus:**

- A one-unit pH decrease means roughly ten times more hydrogen ions.
- Buffers resist change; they do not amplify pH disturbances.

An acid donates hydrogen ions; a base accepts hydrogen ions or increases hydroxide availability in a simple aqueous description. An acid and a base can react to form water and a salt. pH expresses hydrogen ion activity on a logarithmic scale: a decrease of one pH unit represents about a tenfold increase in hydrogen ion concentration.

At approximately room temperature, pure water has pH 7 and equal hydrogen and hydroxide concentrations. Below 7 is acidic; above 7 is alkaline. Acetic acid solutions are acidic, distilled water is approximately neutral before gas absorption, blood is mildly alkaline, and ammonia solutions are alkaline. Physiological arterial blood pH is normally about 7.35–7.45; other body compartments differ. Do not assume stomach contents have blood's pH.

A buffer limits a change in pH by reversibly binding or releasing hydrogen ions; it does not prevent every change. The carbonic acid/bicarbonate relationship is CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3−. Added hydrogen can combine with bicarbonate; carbonic acid can dissociate when hydrogen is removed. Lungs regulate CO2, and kidneys regulate acid excretion and bicarbonate. Buffering, ventilation, and kidney function cooperate.

## 2.6 Carbohydrates and their building blocks

`ch2-carbohydrates`

**Focus:**

- Distinguish monosaccharides, disaccharides, and polysaccharides with examples.
- Starch is plant storage; glycogen is animal storage.

Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. Monosaccharides are single sugars: glucose, fructose, and galactose are familiar examples. Ribose and deoxyribose are five-carbon sugars in nucleic acids. Disaccharides contain two sugar units: sucrose combines glucose and fructose, lactose glucose and galactose, and maltose two glucose units. Hydrolysis separates them into smaller sugars.

Polysaccharides contain many linked monosaccharides. Starch stores glucose in plants; glycogen stores glucose in animals, particularly liver and skeletal muscle. Liver glycogen helps maintain blood glucose, whereas muscle glycogen fuels muscle activity. Cellulose forms plant structural material; humans do not digest its main linkages with their own digestive enzymes, so it contributes dietary fiber.

Sugar identity and linkage matter: compounds containing the same elements can have different structures and functions. Glucose oxidation can supply energy for ATP production, but a sugar is not itself an enzyme or a nucleic acid. Glycoproteins and glycolipids contain carbohydrate components that contribute to cell recognition. Associate each carbohydrate with its number of units, source, and physiological role rather than memorizing only a formula.

## 2.7 Lipids: stored fuel, membranes, and signaling

`ch2-lipids`

**Focus:**

- Compare triglyceride, phospholipid, and steroid structure with function.
- Unsaturation means carbon–carbon double bonds.

Triglycerides consist of glycerol joined to three fatty acids. They store concentrated energy, insulate, and cushion. Saturated fatty acids have no carbon–carbon double bonds in their hydrocarbon chains; unsaturated fatty acids have one or more. Cis double bonds commonly introduce bends, reducing close packing. Physical state depends on composition and temperature, so avoid an absolute rule that every animal fat is solid or every plant fat liquid.

Phospholipids have a polar head and nonpolar fatty acid tails. Their amphipathic structure promotes bilayers, with heads toward water and tails away from it. Steroids have fused carbon rings; cholesterol stabilizes animal membranes and is a precursor of steroid hormones, bile acids, and vitamin D-related molecules. Lipids are not all long repeating polymers in the same sense as proteins or nucleic acids.

Fats provide the body's largest concentrated reserve of metabolic fuel. Breaking down fatty acids feeds energy pathways, while storing triglycerides requires biosynthesis. A molecule's role follows its structure: a phospholipid forms a membrane boundary, while a triglyceride is suited for energy storage. Cholesterol has essential functions even though its transport and concentration have health implications.

## 2.8 Proteins, conformation, and denaturation

`ch2-proteins`

**Focus:**

- Explain primary through quaternary structure without assuming every protein has all four.
- Denaturation changes conformation and can impair function.

Proteins contain amino acids joined by peptide bonds. Each amino acid has an amino group, carboxyl group, hydrogen, and variable side chain attached to a central carbon. Peptide formation links one amino acid's carboxyl region to another's amino region. Sequence determines the possibilities for folding and function.

Primary structure is amino acid sequence. Secondary structure includes alpha helices and beta sheets stabilized by backbone hydrogen bonding. Tertiary structure is a polypeptide's overall three-dimensional shape; quaternary structure combines multiple polypeptide subunits. Hemoglobin has several subunits, while not every protein has a quaternary level.

Functions include catalysis by enzymes, transport, structural support, movement, receptors, signaling, defense, and fluid balance. Examples are collagen, keratin, hemoglobin, antibodies, actin, and membrane channels. A changed shape can impair an active site or binding region. **Denaturation** disrupts normal conformation through heat, extreme pH, or other conditions; it usually does not mean hydrolyzing every peptide bond. Some changes are reversible and others are not. Protein function depends on both sequence and the environment in which folding occurs.

## 2.9 Nucleotides, DNA, RNA, and ATP

`ch2-nucleic`

**Focus:**

- DNA: deoxyribose and thymine; RNA: ribose and uracil.
- Hydrogen bonds connect complementary DNA bases; covalent bonds form the backbone.

A nucleotide contains a nitrogenous base, a five-carbon sugar, and phosphate. DNA uses deoxyribose and the bases adenine, thymine, cytosine, and guanine. RNA uses ribose and uracil instead of thymine. DNA commonly forms two antiparallel strands; RNA commonly occurs as a single strand that may fold into complex shapes.

In DNA, adenine pairs with thymine and cytosine with guanine through hydrogen bonding. Covalent bonds hold each sugar-phosphate backbone together. DNA stores inherited instructions. Messenger RNA carries a transcribed message, ribosomal RNA contributes to ribosomes, and transfer RNA connects codons with amino acids during protein synthesis. DNA occurs mainly in the nucleus but also in mitochondria; RNA is present in nucleus and cytoplasm.

ATP, adenosine triphosphate, contains adenine, ribose, and three phosphates. Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate can be coupled to cellular work; adding phosphate to ADP requires energy. ATP is an immediate energy-transfer molecule, not the largest long-term fuel store. Distinguish nucleotide components from amino acids and distinguish genetic information storage from translation into a polypeptide.
