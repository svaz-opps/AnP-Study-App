## 12.1 Receptors, sensation, perception, and adaptation

`ch12-receptors`

**Focus:**

- Distinguish detection/transduction from conscious interpretation.
- Classify receptors by stimulus and compare adaptation patterns.

A sensory receptor converts stimulus energy into a cellular signal, a process called transduction. Sensation includes detection of a stimulus and its representation in neural pathways; perception is conscious interpretation. Most conscious somatic perception involves cerebral cortex, while many sensory signals support reflexes without becoming conscious.

Mechanoreceptors respond to deformation, thermoreceptors to temperature, chemoreceptors to chemicals, photoreceptors to light, and nociceptors to potentially damaging stimuli. Proprioceptors monitor position and movement. Receptor location can be exteroceptive, interoceptive, or proprioceptive. Classification by stimulus differs from classification by location.

Adaptation is reduced response during sustained stimulation. Rapidly adapting receptors emphasize change; slowly adapting receptors retain information about sustained conditions. Nociceptors generally adapt little or slowly and can sensitize during inflammation; the course shorthand that pain receptors never adapt should not be treated as a universal physiological law. Stimulus intensity can be encoded through firing rate and recruitment, rather than changing every action potential's amplitude. Receptor identity and pathway determine modality, so the same electrical signaling language can represent different experiences depending on where it originates.

## 12.2 Somatic sensation, pain, and proprioception

`ch12-somatic`

**Focus:**

- Contrast muscle length sensing by spindles with tension sensing by tendon organs.
- Explain referred pain and separate fast/slow fibers from acute/chronic duration.

Somatic senses include touch/pressure, temperature, pain, and position/movement. Free endings detect several modalities including pain and temperature. Tactile corpuscles detect changing light touch; lamellated corpuscles detect vibration and changing deep pressure. Merkel-associated receptors and other endings add sustained touch information. Warm and cold receptor populations respond over different temperature ranges; extreme temperatures can activate nociceptors.

Fast, sharp pain commonly uses myelinated A-delta fibers; slower, diffuse pain commonly uses unmyelinated C fibers. Acute versus chronic pain also describes duration and clinical context, so those categories are not identical to fast versus slow fiber classes. Referred pain is perceived away from its source, in part because visceral and somatic signals converge on shared central circuits. Endogenous opioid systems can modulate pain transmission.

Muscle spindles detect muscle length and stretch, while Golgi tendon organs monitor tension near muscle-tendon junctions. Their information helps coordinate posture and force. A rhizotomy interrupts selected nerve-root input and may reduce selected pain transmission but also risks sensory/motor consequences depending on the target; it is not a general cure or a treatment recommendation here. Always distinguish receptor location, stimulus, and central interpretation.

## 12.3 Olfaction and gustation

`ch12-chemical`

**Focus:**

- Trace smell through receptor cilia, bulb, and tract.
- Identify five taste qualities and cranial nerves VII, IX, and X without a rigid tongue map.

Olfaction detects odorants dissolved in mucus around olfactory receptor neuron cilia in the superior nasal region. Axons pass through the cribriform plate to olfactory bulbs, then travel through tracts to primary olfactory and associated limbic/cortical areas. Smell has distinctive early pathways that do not require the usual initial thalamic relay before primary cortex. Adaptation and central habituation reduce awareness of some persistent odors.

Taste receptor cells in taste buds respond to dissolved substances at apical microvilli. Major taste qualities include sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and umami. Different qualities are not confined to four exclusive tongue zones; the traditional rigid tongue map is inaccurate. Taste buds are associated with selected tongue papillae and other oral/pharyngeal sites.

Taste signals travel mainly through facial nerve VII from anterior tongue, glossopharyngeal IX from posterior tongue, and vagus X from selected epiglottic/pharyngeal regions. Brainstem relays project through thalamus to gustatory cortex in insula/frontal operculum. Trigeminal V carries much oral somatic sensation, including texture and irritation, rather than the principal taste pathway. Flavor combines taste, smell, texture, temperature, and experience. Loss of smell can therefore greatly reduce flavor even with functioning taste receptors.

## 12.4 External, middle, and inner ear

`ch12-ear`

**Focus:**

- Trace auricle → canal → tympanic membrane → ossicles → oval window.
- Distinguish middle-ear air space, perilymph, and endolymph.

The external ear includes auricle and auditory canal, directing sound toward the tympanic membrane. The middle ear is an air-filled cavity containing malleus, incus, and stapes. The stapes transmits vibration to the oval window. The auditory/pharyngotympanic tube connects middle ear to nasopharynx and supports pressure equalization. The round window permits pressure displacement within cochlear fluids.

The inner ear includes bony labyrinth surrounding membranous labyrinth. Perilymph occupies spaces outside the membranous labyrinth; endolymph fills it. The cochlea supports hearing, while vestibule and semicircular canals support equilibrium. Utricle and saccule are membranous structures within the vestibule; semicircular ducts lie within bony canals.

Middle-ear muscles reduce ossicular movement during the acoustic/tympanic reflex. This can attenuate selected sustained sounds but has latency and does not reliably protect against all sudden loud impulses. Conductive hearing loss interferes with mechanical transfer; sensorineural loss involves hair cells, neural pathways, or related sensory structures. Age-related hearing decline is often called presbycusis. Keep the fluid-filled inner ear separate from the air-filled middle-ear space.

## 12.5 Cochlear transduction and auditory pathways

`ch12-hearing`

**Focus:**

- Locate scalae, basilar membrane, hair cells, and tectorial membrane.
- Trace mechanical vibration to CN VIII signaling and temporal cortical processing.

The cochlea contains scala vestibuli and scala tympani with perilymph, and a cochlear duct/scala media containing endolymph. Vestibular membrane separates scala vestibuli from the duct; basilar membrane separates the duct from scala tympani. The spiral organ or organ of Corti rests on the basilar membrane; hair cells interact mechanically with surrounding structures including the tectorial membrane.

Sound vibration moves the oval window, generating fluid pressure waves. Basilar membrane movement bends hair-cell stereocilia, opening mechanically sensitive channels and changing transmitter release onto sensory fibers. Cochlear hair cells are mechanoreceptors, not photoreceptors. High frequencies preferentially excite the stiffer cochlear base; lower frequencies reach maximal displacement nearer the apex.

Signals travel in the cochlear branch of cranial nerve VIII through brainstem auditory relays, midbrain inferior colliculus, thalamic medial geniculate region, and auditory cortex in temporal lobe. Central pathways contain bilateral connections, so a single central lesion does not always cause simple complete deafness in one ear. Distinguish mechanical conduction of sound energy from neural conduction after transduction, and distinguish a cochlear hair cell from an axon in the auditory nerve.

## 12.6 Vestibular equilibrium and head movement

`ch12-balance`

**Focus:**

- Maculae detect gravity/linear acceleration; cristae detect angular acceleration.
- Trace vestibular input through CN VIII and central balance/gaze circuits.

The utricle and saccule contain maculae with hair cells embedded in a gelatinous otolithic membrane containing calcium carbonate otoconia. Gravity and linear acceleration shift this mass, bending sensory projections. These structures inform the CNS about head orientation and linear movement, traditionally grouped under static equilibrium.

Each semicircular duct has an enlarged ampulla containing a crista ampullaris. Hair cells project into a gelatinous cupula. Angular acceleration causes relative endolymph movement that deflects the cupula. The three canal orientations support detection of rotation in different planes. At constant prolonged rotation, responses adapt; abrupt stopping can produce a transient opposite sensation.

Vestibular fibers travel through cranial nerve VIII to vestibular nuclei and cerebellar circuits, with connections to eye-movement, postural, and conscious perceptual pathways. The vestibulo-ocular reflex stabilizes gaze during head movement. Balance also relies on vision and proprioception, not inner-ear signals alone. Differentiate macular otolith displacement during linear acceleration from ampullary cupula bending during angular acceleration; both use hair-cell mechanotransduction but are specialized for different stimuli.

## 12.7 Eye protection, tunics, and chambers

`ch12-eye`

**Focus:**

- Identify fibrous, vascular, and neural tunics and their components.
- Distinguish aqueous-filled chambers from the vitreous cavity.

Eyebrows, lids, lashes, conjunctiva, extraocular muscles, and the lacrimal apparatus protect and position the eye. Tears lubricate and contain substances including lysozyme, an enzyme that helps disrupt selected bacterial cell walls. Conjunctiva lines eyelids and covers anterior sclera but does not cover the transparent cornea as a separate conjunctival layer.

The fibrous tunic consists of sclera and transparent cornea. The vascular tunic includes choroid, ciliary body, and iris. The neural tunic includes retina. The pupil is an opening in the iris, not a solid lens. Cornea and lens refract light; iris changes the aperture; choroid supports retinal metabolism and absorbs stray light.

The anterior cavity contains aqueous humor, with an anterior chamber between cornea and iris and a posterior chamber between iris and lens. The much larger posterior/vitreous cavity lies behind the lens and contains vitreous humor. Do not confuse posterior chamber with posterior cavity. Aqueous humor circulates from the ciliary region through the pupil and drains at the anterior chamber angle. An ophthalmoscope permits inspection of internal structures such as retina and optic disc.

## 12.8 Accommodation, photoreceptors, and visual pathways

`ch12-vision`

**Focus:**

- Near focus: ciliary contraction, reduced zonular tension, rounder lens.
- Rods favor dim light; cones favor color/acuity; ganglion axons form CN II.

For near focusing, ciliary muscle contracts, zonular/suspensory tension decreases, and the lens becomes rounder with greater refractive power. For distant focus, ciliary muscle relaxes, zonular tension increases, and the lens flattens. Accommodation changes lens shape rather than moving the retina forward. Cataract is lens clouding; presbyopia is reduced near accommodation with age. They are different processes.

Rods are highly sensitive in dim light and contribute limited color/detail discrimination. Cones support color and high acuity in brighter light and are concentrated in the fovea. The optic disc lacks photoreceptors where retinal axons exit, creating the physiological blind spot. Light passes cornea → aqueous humor → pupil → lens → vitreous → retina. Retinal signaling involves photoreceptors, bipolar cells, and ganglion cells; ganglion cell axons form the optic nerve.

At the optic chiasm, nasal retinal fibers cross while temporal fibers remain ipsilateral. Pathways continue through optic tracts, thalamic lateral geniculate relays, optic radiations, and occipital visual cortex. Pupil reflex pathways branch to other circuits. Explain the difference between the light path through transparent tissues and the neural path after retinal transduction; photons do not travel along the optic nerve as the visual signal.

## 12.9 Visual pigments, adaptation, and sensory aging

`ch12-phototransduction`

**Focus:**

- Light hyperpolarizes photoreceptors and changes their transmitter release.
- Distinguish presbyopia, cataract, glaucoma, retinal disease, and age-related hearing loss.

In darkness, cGMP-gated channels help maintain a relatively depolarized photoreceptor and ongoing transmitter release. Light changes retinal within an opsin pigment, triggering a signaling cascade that reduces cGMP and closes channels. Photoreceptors hyperpolarize and change glutamate release. This receptor potential affects bipolar and ganglion circuits; the photoreceptor itself does not send a conventional axonal action potential down the optic nerve.

Rhodopsin is the rod pigment. Three cone classes have overlapping sensitivities commonly described as short-, medium-, and long-wavelength sensitive; they do not correspond to rigid single-color detectors. Dark adaptation increases sensitivity over time, while adaptation to bright conditions changes photoreceptor and neural responses. Binocular disparity gives the brain one cue for depth perception; vision uses additional depth cues as well.

Presbyopia reduces near accommodation as lens properties change. Cataract clouds the lens. Glaucoma involves optic nerve damage and is often associated with elevated intraocular pressure, though pressure alone does not define every case. Retinal detachment separates neural retina from its supporting tissues. Macular degeneration affects central retinal function; floaters reflect vitreous-related shadows and have different causes. Hearing changes with age can involve cochlear and neural processes; tinnitus is perceived sound without an equivalent external sound source and is not synonymous with every hearing loss.
