## 7.1 The Skeletal System at a Glance

`ch7-overview`

**Focus:**

- The adult skeleton has 206 bones divided into **axial** and **appendicular** portions.
- The girdles (pectoral and pelvic) belong to the **appendicular** skeleton.

The skeletal system is made of **bones** plus the structures that connect them: ligaments, tendons, and cartilages. The adult skeleton has **206 bones**, organized into two divisions:

- The **axial skeleton**: skull, hyoid bone, vertebral column, and thoracic cage (the body's central axis).
- The **appendicular skeleton**: the upper and lower limbs plus the **pectoral (shoulder) girdle** and **pelvic (hip) girdle** that attach the limbs to the trunk.

Bones are living organs. They contain bone tissue, cartilage, dense connective tissue, blood, nerves, and bone marrow.

The scapula is part of the pectoral girdle, which belongs to the appendicular skeleton. The hyoid, sternum (thoracic cage), and sacrum (vertebral column) are all axial.

</details>

## 7.2 Classification of Bones by Shape

`ch7-shape`

**Focus:**

- The **femur** is a long bone; **carpals and tarsals** (wrists and ankles) are short bones.
- The **patella** is a sesamoid bone (some anatomists call it this because it forms inside the quadriceps tendon).
- Vertebrae are irregular bones; skull bones, ribs, sternum, and scapulae are flat bones.

Bones are classified by **shape**:

| Shape | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Long** | A shaft (longer than it is wide) with two expanded ends | Femur (thigh), humerus (arm), radius, ulna, phalanges |
| **Short** | Cube-like; about as long as they are wide | Carpals (wrist), tarsals (ankle) |
| **Flat** | Thin, platelike, usually curved | Most skull bones (frontal, parietal), sternum, scapulae, ribs |
| **Irregular** | Complex shapes that are not long, short, or flat | Vertebrae, auditory ossicles, some facial bones |
| **Sesamoid** | Small, round bones that develop **within a tendon** | **Patella** (kneecap) |
| **Sutural (Wormian)** | Tiny extra bones in the sutures between skull bones | Found in some skulls along the lambdoid suture |

> **Study tip** (tip)
>
> "Long" refers to **shape**, not size. The tiny bones of the fingers (phalanges) are long bones because each has a shaft and two ends.

## 7.3 Parts of a Long Bone

`ch7-long-bone`

**Focus:**

- The **diaphysis** is made mostly of **compact bone**.
- The **epiphyses** are made mainly of **spongy bone**.
- The ends of long bones (articular surfaces) are covered by **hyaline cartilage**.
- The **epiphyseal disk (plate)** on an X-ray tells you a child's bone is still growing.

> 🖼 **Figure — Figure 7.A. Major parts of a long bone (femur), shown in longitudinal section.** ([image](https://lsc-nh-biol2401-final-study-guide.netlify.app/public/figures/longbone.webp))
> Description: The drawing shows a long bone cut lengthwise. Each end is an expanded epiphysis made mostly of spongy bone, capped with articular cartilage where it forms a joint. The long shaft between the ends is the diaphysis: a thick collar of compact bone around a hollow medullary cavity that holds yellow marrow. A thin epiphyseal line (the remnant of the growth plate) separates each epiphysis from the diaphysis. The periosteum covers the outside of the bone except the articular cartilage, and the endosteum lines the medullary cavity. A nutrient foramen lets blood vessels enter the shaft.

| Part | Description | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Diaphysis** | The shaft. A thick collar of **compact bone** around the medullary cavity | Rigidity and strength |
| **Epiphysis** (pl. epiphyses) | The expanded ends: mostly **spongy bone** with a thin outer layer of compact bone; proximal and distal | Hold red marrow (blood cell formation); form joints |
| **Articular cartilage** | A pad of **hyaline cartilage** on the epiphysis where the bone joins another bone | Shock absorber; reduces friction in the joint |
| **Epiphyseal plate / line** | Plate (disk) of hyaline cartilage between epiphysis and diaphysis in a growing bone; becomes the **epiphyseal line** when growth stops | Site of lengthwise growth |
| **Periosteum** | Tough, fibrous outer covering (except over articular cartilage); richly supplied with blood vessels, lymph vessels, and nerves; inner osteogenic layer has osteoblasts and osteoclasts | Protection, nourishment, bone formation and repair; attachment for tendons and ligaments |
| **Endosteum** | Thin inner lining of the medullary cavity with osteoblasts and osteoclasts | Bone remodeling |
| **Medullary cavity** | Hollow center of the diaphysis, filled with **yellow marrow** in adults | Fat (energy) storage; makes bone lighter |
| **Nutrient foramen** | Perforating canal through the compact bone | Lets blood vessels enter and leave the bone |

## 7.4 Bone Marrow and Hematopoiesis

`ch7-marrow`

**Focus:**

- **Yellow marrow stores fat.**
- **Red marrow** forms red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets (hematopoiesis).
- In adults, **red marrow is found primarily in spongy bone**.

Bone marrow is the soft tissue inside the medullary cavities of long bones and the spaces of spongy bone.

| Characteristic | **Red bone marrow** | **Yellow bone marrow** |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | **Hematopoiesis**: forms red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets | **Stores fat** (an energy reserve) |
| Where in adults | **Spongy bone** of flat bones (skull, ribs, sternum, clavicles, vertebrae, pelvis) and the proximal epiphyses of the femur and humerus | Medullary cavity of the long bones' shafts |
| Where in infants | Nearly all bones | Little |

As a person ages, much of the red marrow is replaced by yellow marrow. In a flat bone, the arrangement looks like a sandwich: a layer of **spongy bone with red marrow** (the meat) between two layers of **compact bone** (the bread).

> **Clinical connection** (clinical)
>
> In an emergency or for a marrow biopsy, red marrow is often sampled from the **sternum** or the **iliac crest** of the hip bone because these flat bones keep active red marrow throughout life.

## 7.5 Bone Matrix and Bone Cells

`ch7-cells`

**Focus:**

- **Osteoclasts** break down (resorb) bone tissue.
- **Osteocytes** live inside **lacunae**.
- Most of the bone matrix is made of **hydroxyapatite** crystals (calcium phosphate).

Bone tissue is a connective tissue: cells surrounded by an extracellular **matrix**.

**Matrix composition** - **Organic part (about 35%)**: mostly **collagen** fibers (called osteoid when newly laid down). Collagen gives bone **flexibility and tensile strength** so it does not shatter. - **Inorganic part (about 65%)**: mineral salts, mainly calcium phosphate in the form of **hydroxyapatite** crystals. Hydroxyapatite gives bone its **hardness**.

**Bone cells**

| Cell | Where it comes from | Function | Memory trick |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Osteoprogenitor cell** | Stem cell from mesenchyme | Divides (mitosis) and becomes osteoblasts | "Pro-" = before |
| **Osteoblast** | From osteoprogenitor cells | **Builds** bone: secretes collagen and matrix; cannot divide | **B**last = **B**uild |
| **Osteocyte** | A mature osteoblast that became trapped in matrix | Maintains bone; **lives inside a lacuna**; exchanges nutrients and wastes with blood | **C**yte = **C**aretaker |
| **Osteoclast** | Large, multinucleated cell (from white blood cell line) | **Breaks down (resorbs)** bone matrix, releasing calcium and phosphate into the blood | **C**last = **C**ut / **C**rush |

Osteoclasts secrete **acids** that dissolve the calcium salts and **lysosomal enzymes** that digest the collagen.

> Description: Study the labeled structures in Bone cells. The accompanying section explains their relationships: Osteoclasts break down (resorb) bone tissue. Osteocytes live inside lacunae. Most of the bone matrix is made of hydroxyapatite crystals (calcium phosphate).

## 7.6 Compact and Spongy Bone

`ch7-compact-spongy`

**Focus:**

- **Spongy** bone = **cancellous** bone.
- **Compact** bone forms most of the **diaphysis** and is built of **osteons** (concentric circles).
- The **epiphyses** are composed mainly of **spongy** bone.

> 🖼 **Figure — Figure 7.B. Microscopic structure of compact bone: an osteon (Haversian system).** ([image](https://lsc-nh-biol2401-final-study-guide.netlify.app/public/figures/osteon.webp))
> Description: The illustration enlarges a block of compact bone from the shaft of a long bone. Circular osteons contain concentric lamellae around central canals carrying blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, and nerves. A perforating canal crosses between the central canals and connects them with vessels beneath the periosteum. Circumferential lamellae follow the outer surface, while interstitial lamellae fill spaces between osteons. The right side shows the transition to trabeculae of spongy bone and the medullary cavity. Osteocytes, lacunae, and canaliculi are discussed in the accompanying text but are not individually labeled in this illustration.

| Feature | **Compact bone** | **Spongy bone** (also called **cancellous** bone) |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance | Solid, dense, smooth | Lattice of thin bony plates with open spaces |
| Structural unit | The **osteon** (Haversian system): concentric circles (lamellae) around a central canal | **Trabeculae**: needle-like bony plates, no osteons |
| Where | Wall of the **diaphysis**; thin outer layer over all bones | **Epiphyses** of long bones; inside flat, short, and irregular bones |
| Nourishment | Blood vessels in central and perforating canals; osteocytes connected by canaliculi | Diffusion from nearby canals and marrow |
| Advantage | Strength and support | Light weight; holds red marrow; trabeculae line up along lines of stress |

**Parts of an osteon** - **Central (Haversian) canal**: contains blood vessels and nerves. - **Lamellae**: concentric layers of matrix. - **Lacunae**: small chambers holding osteocytes. - **Canaliculi**: tiny canals that connect lacunae so osteocytes can pass nutrients and wastes. - **Perforating (Volkmann's) canals**: run at right angles and connect the blood supply of neighboring osteons.

## 7.7 How Bones Form: Ossification

`ch7-ossification`

**Focus:**

- The **frontal bone** (a flat skull bone) forms by **intramembranous** ossification; the femur, ulna, and radius form by endochondral ossification.
- Know the steps of endochondral ossification and recognize the false one (cartilage does not become adipose tissue).

**Ossification** (osteogenesis) is the formation of bone. It begins at about 6 to 7 weeks of embryonic development. In both patterns, an existing connective tissue is **replaced** by bone.

**1. Intramembranous ossification**: bone forms **within sheet-like layers of fibrous connective tissue membranes**. - Forms the **flat bones of the skull** (frontal, parietal, occipital, temporal) and the clavicles. - Connective tissue cells become osteoblasts, which deposit spongy bone; the outside becomes compact bone and periosteum.

**2. Endochondral ossification**: bone forms from a **hyaline cartilage model**. It forms **most bones** of the skeleton, including the femur, humerus, radius, ulna, and vertebrae.

Steps of endochondral ossification: 1. **Hyaline cartilage** develops in the shape of the future bone. 2. The cartilage cells in the center enlarge and the matrix around them calcifies, so the cells die. 3. **Periosteum forms** from connective tissue on the outside of the developing bone. 4. Blood vessels and osteoblasts invade; the **primary ossification center** appears in the diaphysis. **Osteoblasts deposit bone tissue in place of the disintegrating cartilage.** 5. **Secondary ossification centers** later appear in the epiphyses. 6. Cartilage remains only as **articular cartilage** and the **epiphyseal plates**, which allow growth.

> **Note** (note)
>
> Cartilage is replaced by **bone**, never by adipose (fat) tissue. A statement such as "hyaline cartilage changes to adipose tissue" is **not** a step of endochondral ossification.

## 7.8 How Bones Grow in Length and Thickness

`ch7-growth`

**Focus:**

- All bones are normally ossified by about **25 years** of age.
- You can tell a child's long bone is growing by looking at the **epiphyseal disk** on an X-ray.
- A bone grows in **thickness** by **deposition of compact bone beneath the periosteum**.

**Growth in length (longitudinal growth)** happens at the **epiphyseal plate** (epiphyseal disk), a plate of hyaline cartilage between the epiphysis and diaphysis. It has four zones:

1. **Zone of resting cartilage**: small, scattered chondrocytes anchor the plate to the epiphysis.
2. **Zone of proliferating cartilage**: chondrocytes divide and stack like coins.
3. **Zone of hypertrophic cartilage**: large, maturing chondrocytes in columns.
4. **Zone of calcified cartilage**: a thin layer of dead cells in calcified matrix; osteoclasts break it down and osteoblasts replace it with bone.

New cartilage is made on the epiphyseal side while cartilage is replaced by bone on the diaphyseal side. The plate stays about the same thickness while the **bone lengthens**.

When growth ends, the plate ossifies and leaves an **epiphyseal line**. Most epiphyseal plates close between ages 14 and 20, and **ossification of all bones is normally complete by about age 25**.

> **Clinical connection** (clinical)
>
> On an **X-ray of a child**, the epiphyseal disk appears as a dark (cartilage) line near the end of the bone. If you can see it, the bone is **still growing**. In an adult, only a thin epiphyseal line remains.

**Growth in thickness (appositional growth)** - **Osteoblasts beneath the periosteum deposit compact bone** on the outer surface. - At the same time, **osteoclasts** on the endosteal (inner) surface break down bone, enlarging the medullary cavity. - The bone becomes wider without becoming too heavy.

## 7.9 Factors That Affect Bone Growth

`ch7-factors`

**Focus:**

- **Vitamin C** is required for collagen synthesis.
- **Excess GH before the epiphyseal plates ossify → pituitary gigantism** (after closure → acromegaly).
- **Excess thyroid hormone can halt bone growth** by causing **premature ossification of the epiphyseal disks**.
- **Sex hormones stimulate ossification of the epiphyseal disks, with estrogen stronger than androgens.**
- Increased bone mass from physical stress = bone **hypertrophy**.

**Minerals**: **calcium** and **phosphorus** (parts of hydroxyapatite); magnesium (osteoblast activity); boron and manganese.

**Vitamins**

| Vitamin | Role | Deficiency |
|---|---|---|
| **Vitamin D** | Increases absorption of calcium from the small intestine | **Rickets** (children), **osteomalacia** (adults): soft, weak bones |
| **Vitamin C** | Required for **collagen synthesis** (maintains bone matrix) | **Scurvy**: weak, slow-healing bones |
| **Vitamin A** | Needed for osteoblast and osteoclast activity during development | Retarded bone development |
| **Vitamin B12** | May help osteoblast activity |  |

**Hormones**

| Hormone | Effect on bone |
|---|---|
| **Growth hormone (GH)** from the pituitary | Stimulates division of cartilage cells in the epiphyseal plates. **Too little** in childhood causes **pituitary dwarfism**. **Too much before the plates ossify** causes **pituitary gigantism**. **Too much in adults** (plates already closed) causes **acromegaly** (bones of hands, feet, and jaw thicken). |
| **Thyroid hormones** (thyroxine) | Stimulate replacement of cartilage by bone. An **excess can halt bone growth** by causing **premature ossification of the epiphyseal disks**. |
| **Sex hormones** (estrogens and androgens such as testosterone) | Promote osteoblast activity and stimulate **ossification of the epiphyseal disks**, which ends growth. **Estrogen's effect is stronger than androgens'**, so the plates of females usually close earlier. |
| **Parathyroid hormone (PTH)** and **calcitonin** | Control bone remodeling and blood calcium (see Bone Remodeling). |

**Physical stress (exercise)** - Weight-bearing activity and the pull of muscles **stimulate bone growth**: the bone becomes thicker and stronger. This increase in bone mass is called **bone hypertrophy**. - Lack of use causes **bone atrophy** (bone loss), as in a person on prolonged bed rest or an astronaut in low gravity.

### ✅ Check yourself

- A. Gigantism, because his epiphyseal plates are still open
- B. Acromegaly, because his epiphyseal plates have already ossified
- C. Rickets, from lack of vitamin D
- D. Dwarfism, from lack of growth hormone

Excess growth hormone after the epiphyseal plates have closed cannot make bones longer, so bones thicken instead: acromegaly. Before the plates close, the same excess would cause gigantism.

</details>

## 7.10 Functions of Bones

`ch7-functions`

**Focus:**

- Production of blood cells, storage of inorganic salts, **and** protection of organs are all skeletal functions: the answer "all of the above."

1. **Support**: bones of the legs and pelvis support the trunk; the atlas supports the skull.
2. **Protection**: the skull protects the brain; the rib cage protects the heart and lungs. Protection is especially important in the **head and thorax**.
3. **Body movement**: bones act as **levers**; skeletal muscles attach to them by tendons.
4. **Blood cell formation (hematopoiesis)**: red marrow produces red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets.
5. **Storage of inorganic salts**: bone stores calcium and phosphate (and smaller amounts of magnesium, sodium, potassium) and releases them as needed. This is essential for calcium homeostasis.
6. **Energy storage**: yellow marrow stores fat.

> **Memory trick** (mnemonic)
>
> **"SPM-BSE"**, or think **"Some People Move Blood, Salts, and Energy"**: Support, Protection, Movement, Blood cell formation, Salt storage, Energy storage.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Bone releasing calcium into the blood when dietary calcium is low**

Bone stores inorganic salts, especially calcium and phosphate, and releases them into the blood to maintain homeostasis.

## 7.11 Bone Remodeling and Calcium Homeostasis

`ch7-remodeling`

**Focus:**

- The **thyroid gland** releases **calcitonin**, which causes osteoblasts to deposit calcium in bone.
- When the parathyroid glands sense **low blood calcium**, they release **parathyroid hormone**.
- **High blood calcium inhibits osteoclast activity** (through calcitonin).

Bone is **continuously remodeled** throughout life. **Osteoclasts** remove bone (resorption) and **osteoblasts** build new bone. The rate varies: part of the distal femur is replaced about every four months, while parts of the diaphysis may never be fully replaced.

Two hormones control remodeling and the **calcium level in the blood** by **negative feedback**:

> 🖼 **Figure — Figure 7.C. Negative feedback control of blood calcium by calcitonin and parathyroid hormone.** ([image](https://lsc-nh-biol2401-final-study-guide.netlify.app/public/figures/calcium.webp))
> Description: The diagram is a loop centered on normal blood calcium. On the high side: when blood calcium rises, the thyroid gland releases calcitonin. Calcitonin inhibits osteoclasts, osteoblasts deposit calcium into bone, and the kidneys excrete more calcium, so blood calcium falls back to normal. On the low side: when blood calcium falls, the parathyroid glands release parathyroid hormone. Parathyroid hormone stimulates osteoclasts to resorb bone, the kidneys reabsorb calcium, and the intestine absorbs more calcium (with vitamin D), so blood calcium rises back to normal.

| Characteristic | **Calcitonin** | **PTH (parathyroid hormone)** |
|---|---|---|
| Gland | **Thyroid gland** | **Parathyroid glands** |
| Stimulus | **High** blood calcium | **Low** blood calcium |
| Osteoclasts | **Inhibited** | **Stimulated** (bone resorption releases Ca²⁺) |
| Osteoblasts | Stimulated to **deposit calcium** in bone | — |
| Kidneys | Excrete more Ca²⁺ in urine | Reabsorb Ca²⁺ back into blood |
| Intestine | — | Absorbs more dietary Ca²⁺ (with vitamin D) |
| Result | Blood calcium **decreases** to normal | Blood calcium **increases** to normal |

> **Memory trick** (mnemonic)
>
> Calci**TONE**in **tones down** blood calcium. **PTH** **P**ulls calcium **T**o the **H**eart (into the blood).

### ✅ Check yourself

After a large calcium-rich meal, blood calcium rises above normal. Which response restores balance?

- A. The parathyroid glands secrete more PTH, stimulating osteoclasts
- B. The thyroid releases calcitonin; osteoclasts are inhibited and calcium is deposited in bone
- C. Osteoclasts become more active to store calcium
- D. The kidneys reabsorb more calcium

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The thyroid releases calcitonin; osteoclasts are inhibited and calcium is deposited in bone**

High blood calcium triggers calcitonin from the thyroid. Calcitonin inhibits osteoclasts, so less calcium leaves bone, and osteoblasts deposit calcium, lowering blood calcium.

</details>

## 7.12 Fractures and Fracture Repair

`ch7-fractures`

**Focus:**

- A **compound** fracture: the broken bone is exposed through an opening in the skin.
- Fracture repair order: **hematoma → granulation tissue → callus** (then remodeling).
- A **spiral** fracture is caused by twisting and is often seen in child abuse cases.

A **fracture** is a break in a bone.

| Fracture type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Simple (closed)** | Broken bone does **not** break through the skin |
| **Compound (open)** | Broken bone is **exposed to the outside** through an opening in the skin (high infection risk) |
| **Greenstick** | Incomplete break; the bone bends and breaks on one side only, like a green twig. Common in **children**, whose bones contain more collagen |
| **Fissured** | Incomplete longitudinal crack |
| **Comminuted** | Bone shatters into **several fragments** |
| **Transverse** | Complete break straight **across** the bone |
| **Oblique** | Break at an angle |
| **Spiral** | Caused by **excessive twisting** of a bone; often seen in **child abuse** cases (a twisting injury of a child's arm or leg) |
| **Pathologic** | Caused by disease that weakens the bone (for example, osteoporosis or cancer) |

**Steps of fracture repair** 1. **Hematoma**: blood vessels break and a blood clot forms at the fracture. 2. **Granulation tissue**: fibroblasts and new blood vessels invade the clot; phagocytes remove debris. Some cells become cartilage-forming cells. 3. **Callus**: a cartilaginous callus forms, then osteoblasts replace it with a **bony callus** of spongy bone that bridges the break. 4. **Remodeling**: osteoclasts remove excess bone, and compact bone restores the original shape.

> **Memory trick** (mnemonic)
>
> **"Happy Grandmas Cook Recipes"**: **H**ematoma → **G**ranulation tissue → **C**allus → **R**emodeling.

## 7.13 The Axial Skeleton

`ch7-axial`

**Focus:**

- The **parietal and temporal** bones are joined by a **suture** (the squamous suture).
- **Scoliosis** causes one hip or shoulder to be lower than the other and is most common in adolescent females.

**Skull** (22 bones). All skull bones except the **mandible** are joined by immovable joints called **sutures**.

- **Cranium (8 bones)**: frontal, occipital, sphenoid, ethmoid, **2 parietal**, **2 temporal**.
- Frontal bone: forehead; contains frontal sinuses.
- Parietal bones: bulging sides and top of the skull.
- Occipital bone: back and base of the skull; **foramen magnum** (spinal cord passes through) and **occipital condyles** (articulate with the atlas).
- Temporal bones: sides of the skull; external acoustic meatus, **mastoid process**, styloid process, zygomatic process, mandibular fossa.
- Sphenoid bone: butterfly-shaped "keystone" of the cranial floor; **sella turcica** holds the pituitary gland.
- Ethmoid bone: cribriform plate, crista galli, perpendicular plate (upper nasal septum), nasal conchae.
- **Major sutures**: **coronal** (frontal–parietal), **sagittal** (between parietals), **lambdoid** (parietal–occipital), **squamous** (parietal–temporal).
- **Facial bones (14)**: maxillae, palatine, zygomatic, lacrimal, nasal, vomer, inferior nasal conchae, and the mandible. The **mandible** is the only movable skull bone (temporomandibular joint, **TMJ**).
- **Paranasal sinuses** are in the frontal, sphenoid, ethmoid, and maxillary bones.
- **Infant skull**: **fontanels** ("soft spots") allow the skull to change shape during birth and let the brain grow.

**Hyoid bone**: U-shaped bone in the neck below the mandible; supports the tongue. It does not articulate with any other bone.

**Vertebral column**: 33 vertebrae in an infant; 26 bones in an adult because the sacral and coccygeal vertebrae fuse.

| Region | Number |
|---|---|
| Cervical (neck) | 7 (C1 = atlas, C2 = axis) |
| Thoracic (chest) | 12 (articulate with ribs) |
| Lumbar (lower back) | 5 (largest bodies) |
| Sacrum | 5 fused |
| Coccyx (tailbone) | 3–5 fused |

- **Intervertebral discs** of fibrocartilage between vertebral bodies absorb shock.
- **Curvatures**: thoracic and sacral (primary, present at birth); cervical and lumbar (secondary, develop as the baby holds up the head and stands).

**Abnormal curvatures of the spine**

| Condition | Description |
|---|---|
| **Scoliosis** | **Lateral (sideways) curve**: one hip or shoulder is **lower than the other**. Most common in **adolescent females** |
| **Kyphosis** | Exaggerated thoracic curve ("hunchback") |
| **Lordosis** | Exaggerated lumbar curve ("swayback") |

**Thoracic cage**: 12 pairs of ribs, the sternum, thoracic vertebrae, and costal cartilages. - **True ribs** (pairs 1–7) attach directly to the sternum by costal cartilage. - **False ribs** (pairs 8–12) attach indirectly or not at all. - **Floating ribs** (pairs 11–12) do not attach anteriorly. - **Sternum** has three parts: manubrium (articulates with clavicles), body, and xiphoid process.

## 7.14 The Appendicular Skeleton

`ch7-appendicular`

**Focus:**

- The **pectoral girdle** is made of the **clavicle and scapula** bones.

**Pectoral girdle (shoulder girdle)**: connects the upper limbs to the trunk. It is made of **two clavicles and two scapulae**. - **Clavicle** (collarbone): medial sternal end and lateral acromial end. - **Scapula** (shoulder blade): flat, triangular; **glenoid cavity** (holds the head of the humerus), **acromion** (tip of the shoulder), **coracoid process**, spine.

**Upper limb** (30 bones each) - **Humerus**: head, greater and lesser tubercles, deltoid tuberosity, **capitulum** (joins radius) and **trochlea** (joins ulna), olecranon fossa. - **Radius** (thumb side) and **ulna** (pinky side). The ulna's **olecranon** is the point of the elbow. - **Carpals** (8 wrist bones), **metacarpals** (5 palm bones), **phalanges** (14 finger bones; the thumb, or pollex, has 2).

**Pelvic girdle**: two **coxal (hip) bones**, each formed by the fusion of the **ilium**, **ischium**, and **pubis**. - The coxal bones join each other anteriorly at the **symphysis pubis** (a pad of fibrocartilage) and the sacrum posteriorly. - **Acetabulum**: socket for the head of the femur. **Obturator foramen**: largest foramen in the skeleton. - The **female pelvis** is wider and lighter, the ilia flare more, and the pubic arch is wider (an adaptation for childbirth).

**Lower limb** (30 bones each) - **Femur**: the longest, largest, and strongest bone. Head, **neck**, greater and lesser trochanters, linea aspera, condyles. - **Patella**: sesamoid bone in the quadriceps tendon. - **Tibia** (shinbone; medial malleolus) and **fibula** (lateral, thin; lateral malleolus). - **Tarsals** (7; the **talus** joins the leg bones, and the **calcaneus** is the heel), **metatarsals** (5), **phalanges** (14).

## 7.15 Life-Span Changes and Osteoporosis

`ch7-aging`

**Focus:**

- A **light-skinned woman** is most likely to have the lowest bone mass at age 65.
- The upper femur commonly breaks in the elderly because **trabecular bone weakens sooner than compact bone**.

- Height begins to decrease gradually around age 30 to 35, as intervertebral discs compress.
- **Bone loss exceeds bone formation** with age. After **menopause**, women lose bone faster because estrogen levels fall. By about age 70, bone loss is similar in both sexes.
- Fractures become more common as bones age.

**Osteoporosis** is a disease in which bones lose mass and become porous and fragile. Risk factors: - **Female sex** and **menopause** (loss of estrogen) - **Light-skinned (white or Asian) ancestry**: lighter-skinned people start with less peak bone mass - Small, thin body frame - Low calcium or vitamin D intake, inactivity, smoking, heavy alcohol use

Because of these factors, a **light-skinned woman** is the person most likely to have the lowest bone mass at age 65, compared with a dark-skinned woman or men of similar activity and nutrition.

**Why the upper femur breaks in older adults**: **trabecular (spongy) bone is lost sooner and faster than compact bone**. The femoral **neck** and the vertebral bodies contain a lot of spongy bone, so they weaken first. Hip fractures (fractures of the upper femur), vertebral compression fractures, and wrist fractures are typical.

### ✅ Check yourself

Two 68-year-old patients have the same diet and activity. Who has the higher risk of an osteoporotic hip fracture, and why?

- A. A dark-skinned man, because men have thinner bones
- B. A light-skinned woman, because of lower peak bone mass and estrogen loss after menopause
- C. A dark-skinned woman, because spongy bone is stronger in women
- D. They have equal risk at every age

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A light-skinned woman, because of lower peak bone mass and estrogen loss after menopause**

Light-skinned women start with less bone mass and lose bone faster after menopause. Hip fractures happen where spongy (trabecular) bone is plentiful, such as the femoral neck.

</details>

## 7.16 Other Skeletal Disorders

`ch7-disorders`

| Disorder | Key idea |
|---|---|
| **Sickle cell disease** | Abnormal hemoglobin; red marrow expands, and blocked blood flow can damage bone |
| **Pituitary dwarfism** | Too little growth hormone in childhood; short stature with normal proportions |
| **Osteomalacia / rickets** | Lack of vitamin D (or calcium): bones soften; rickets causes bowed legs in children |
| **Osteogenesis imperfecta** | Inherited defect in collagen ("brittle bone disease") |

| **Cleft palate** | The palatine processes of the maxillae fail to fuse; the oral and nasal cavities stay connected |
| **Cleidocranial dysplasia** | Inherited: clavicles absent or small and skull fontanels slow to close |
| **Polydactyly** | Extra fingers or toes |
| **Herniated disc** | An intervertebral disc ruptures and presses on spinal nerves |
