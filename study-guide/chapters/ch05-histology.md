## 5.1 Four tissue classes and epithelial organization

`ch5-epithelia`

**Focus:**

- Relate epithelial polarity, basement membrane, avascularity, and regeneration.
- Classify stratified tissue by the shape of its apical cells.

Epithelial tissue covers surfaces, lines spaces, and forms glands. Connective tissue supports, binds, protects, stores, and transports through diverse extracellular matrices. Muscle generates force by contraction. Nervous tissue processes and transmits information. Ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm contribute different tissue lineages; epithelium can arise from all three, while most connective/muscle tissue derives from mesoderm and nervous tissue from ectoderm.

Epithelia contain closely packed cells with little extracellular matrix. An apical surface faces a lumen or exterior; the basal surface attaches to a basement membrane above connective tissue. Epithelia are avascular: nutrients diffuse from underlying vascular connective tissue. Many epithelial populations regenerate rapidly. Tight junctions limit leakage, desmosomes resist separation, and gap junctions allow communication.

Classify by layers and the shape of cells at the free surface. Simple means one layer; stratified means multiple layers. Squamous cells are flattened, cuboidal approximately as tall as wide, and columnar taller than wide. A stratified squamous tissue can have rounded basal cells because classification uses its surface cells. Microvilli increase absorptive area; cilia move surface material.

## 5.2 Simple and pseudostratified epithelia

`ch5-simple`

**Focus:**

- Link thin squamous layers to exchange and cuboidal/columnar layers to secretion or absorption.
- Pseudostratified means every cell contacts the basement membrane.

| Tissue | Recognition | Representative location | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple squamous | One thin flattened layer | Alveoli, vessel endothelium, serous mesothelium | Diffusion, filtration, low-friction lining |
| Simple cuboidal | One layer with rounded central nuclei | Kidney tubules, thyroid follicles, small gland ducts | Absorption and secretion |
| Simple columnar | Tall cells, often basal nuclei | Intestine; ciliated type in uterine tubes | Absorption, secretion, or movement |
| Pseudostratified columnar | Uneven nuclei; all cells reach basement membrane | Ciliated respiratory lining in trachea | Mucus secretion and clearance |

Goblet cells secrete mucus, while cilia move mucus and trapped particles. Pseudostratified tissue looks multilayered because nuclei sit at different heights, but it remains a single layer; not every cell reaches the lumen. Do not confuse cilia with microvilli. A thin exchange barrier favors diffusion, whereas multiple layers provide abrasion resistance. Endothelium is the specialized simple squamous lining of blood and lymphatic vessels; mesothelium covers serous surfaces.

## 5.3 Stratified and transitional epithelia

`ch5-stratified`

**Focus:**

- Keratinized stratified squamous forms epidermis; nonkeratinized protects moist surfaces.
- Transitional epithelium stretches while maintaining a urinary barrier.

Stratified squamous epithelium resists abrasion. Its keratinized form constitutes the epidermis, with dead superficial cells rich in keratin. Nonkeratinized forms line moist surfaces such as the mouth, esophagus, and vagina; surface cells retain nuclei. Basal cells replenish cells lost from the surface.

Stratified cuboidal epithelium occurs in portions of sweat gland and other larger ducts. Stratified columnar tissue is less common and appears in selected large ducts and portions of the male urethra. Tissue names describe layers and apical cell shape rather than indicating that all cells have identical shape.

Transitional epithelium, or urothelium, lines urinary structures such as the bladder and ureters. Its surface cells appear rounded or dome-shaped when relaxed and flatten as the wall stretches. Multiple layers maintain a protective barrier while allowing distension. Stretching changes shape without converting it into simple squamous epithelium. Compare a distensible bladder lining with an intestinal absorptive surface: different mechanical demands explain different epithelial arrangements.

## 5.4 Glands, secretion, and epithelial cancer

`ch5-glands`

**Focus:**

- Exocrine uses ducts; endocrine releases hormones without ducts.
- Holocrine loses the entire secretory cell.

Exocrine glands deliver products through ducts onto surfaces or into lumens. Endocrine glands lack ducts and release hormones into interstitial fluid for circulation. A goblet cell is a unicellular exocrine gland. Multicellular glands can be simple or compound according to duct branching and tubular or acinar/alveolar according to secretory unit shape.

Merocrine secretion releases products by exocytosis without losing a major part of the cell. Apocrine secretion releases apical cytoplasm with product in the classical mechanism. Holocrine secretion releases the contents of disintegrating whole cells, as in sebaceous glands. Gland names and secretion mechanisms are not always identical: human apocrine sweat glands largely release products through exocytosis despite their historical name. Mammary secretion includes different mechanisms for lipid and protein components.

Serous secretions are relatively watery and often enzyme-rich; mucous secretions contain mucins and become viscous mucus. Carcinoma is a malignant tumor arising from epithelial cells; the term does not mean every epithelial growth is malignant. Distinguish the gland's destination, duct structure, product, and release mechanism as separate classification dimensions.

## 5.5 Connective tissue cells, fibers, and matrix

`ch5-matrix`

**Focus:**

- Matrix equals ground substance plus fibers; cell types contribute different functions.
- Collagen resists tension, elastic fibers recoil, reticular fibers form a network.

Connective tissues generally contain cells dispersed within extracellular matrix, unlike the tightly packed cells of epithelia. Matrix consists of ground substance plus fibers; its composition explains why blood is fluid, cartilage firm and flexible, and bone rigid. Most connective tissues develop from mesenchyme, an embryonic tissue with relatively unspecialized cells.

Fibroblasts produce ground substance and fibers. Macrophages phagocytose debris and microbes. Mast cells release mediators including histamine that contribute to inflammation. Adipocytes store lipid; plasma cells produce antibodies; other migrating leukocytes participate in defense. A blast cell typically produces matrix; a mature -cyte maintains it, as in osteoblast/osteocyte and chondroblast/chondrocyte.

Collagen fibers resist tension. Elastic fibers stretch and recoil. Reticular fibers form fine supportive networks. Ground substance permits diffusion and varies from fluid to mineralized material. Vascularity differs: cartilage is avascular, tendons have relatively limited perfusion, and bone is richly vascular. Extracellular fluid includes interstitial fluid, plasma, lymph, and specialized transcellular fluids such as cerebrospinal and synovial fluids. Do not assume every connective tissue has equal healing speed.

## 5.6 Loose, dense, and specialized connective tissues

`ch5-connective`

**Focus:**

- Predict function from fiber arrangement and matrix composition.
- Distinguish all three cartilages, compact bone, and blood.

| Type | Key structure | Location and function |
|---|---|---|
| Areolar | Loose fibers, varied cells, hydrated ground substance | Under epithelia; packing and diffusion |
| Adipose | Lipid-filled adipocytes | Hypodermis and around organs; fuel, insulation, cushioning |
| Reticular | Fine branching network | Lymph nodes, spleen, marrow; supportive framework |
| Dense regular | Parallel collagen bundles | Tendons and many ligaments; tension mainly along one axis |
| Dense irregular | Interwoven collagen | Dermis and capsules; stress in several directions |
| Elastic | Abundant elastic fibers | Large arteries and selected ligaments; recoil |
| Hyaline cartilage | Smooth-looking matrix, chondrocytes in lacunae | Articular surfaces, trachea, nose; support/cushioning |
| Elastic cartilage | Elastic fiber-rich cartilage | External ear, epiglottis; flexible support |
| Fibrocartilage | Thick collagen bundles | Intervertebral discs and pubic symphysis; resists compression/tension |
| Bone | Mineralized collagen matrix, osteocytes | Skeleton; support, protection, mineral storage |
| Blood | Cells/fragments in plasma | Transport, defense, hemostasis |

Cartilage heals slowly in part because it lacks blood vessels. Compact bone forms osteons around central canals containing vessels and nerves. Erythrocytes transport gases, leukocytes provide defense, and platelets help stop bleeding. Blood's liquid matrix still makes it a connective tissue. Tendons attach muscle to bone; ligaments generally connect bone to bone.

## 5.7 Tissue membranes, muscle, and nervous tissue

`ch5-membranes`

**Focus:**

- Synovial membranes are connective tissue membranes without true epithelium.
- Compare skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscle by structure, location, and control.

Cutaneous membrane is skin: keratinized epithelium over connective tissue. Mucous membranes line passages opening to the exterior, such as digestive and respiratory tracts, with epithelium supported by lamina propria. Serous membranes line closed ventral body cavities and cover organs with mesothelium over thin connective tissue. Synovial membranes line movable joint capsules and are connective-tissue membranes, lacking a true epithelial layer.

Skeletal muscle has long, cylindrical, striated, multinucleated fibers and normally supports voluntary movement. Cardiac muscle is striated, branched, usually centrally nucleated, involuntary, and joined by intercalated discs; it occurs in the heart. Smooth muscle has spindle-shaped, nonstriated cells with one central nucleus and occurs in walls of hollow organs and vessels. Contractility is the capacity to develop force and shorten under appropriate conditions, although tension can also occur without overall shortening.

Nervous tissue includes neurons and supporting glia in brain, spinal cord, and peripheral nerves. Neurons receive and transmit signals; glia support, protect, and regulate their environment. Repair depends on the tissue, damage, blood supply, and available progenitor cells; scar connective tissue can replace lost structure without fully restoring original function. Astrocytes support the brain vascular environment; specialized capillary endothelial tight junctions form a principal restrictive seal of the blood-brain barrier.
